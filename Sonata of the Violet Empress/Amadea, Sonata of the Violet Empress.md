### Act 0: Weight (Chapter 0)

The last thing her mother gave her was weight.

Not warmth. Warmth had been gone for two days by then.

It sat in her hand like a brittle stone that still remembered heat.

No, never a gentle thing. What she had was the draining of light that follows when the last candle in a fleeing home finally burns out.

Not the warmth of the flame, but what is left behind when everyone has escaped. An attuned, sacrificial, and absolute weight of the shadow it left.

She remembers the third river crossing — or at least a fragment of it, always starting the same way.

One Beat.

A haunting, dancing silhouette in the space behind them.

One became three, three became many. The shadows imprinted on the walls, closing in on her. Laughter, until ecstasy shifted to screams.

Her mother lifted her in one move, away from the crowd that kept growing in euphoria beneath the sound of their steps.

A second Beat.

Her mother stopped carrying her.

They ran together through the forsaken streets scarred by death. Lips moving against her silver hair, mumbling something, desperate, followed by the somber sound of irrational joy those same lips kept biting back.

Then tears came. She was exhausted. She tried to call out to her mother, but the hand dragging her wouldn’t stop.

It was the only inheritance she had left — the void of a hand that had stopped answering, no matter how hard her small fingers clutched it.

A third Beat.

They made a sharp turn towards a river.

A song began amidst the chaos, at first resonant, mellow. Then unraveling into howls and a low humming static. Then a stronger buzzing noise. Then nothing.

Then a deafening screech.

A flash forced them off the ruined road.

Her mother carried her again. She leaped to the lower pass toward a bridge, her silver hair barely dodging a hurled fist of fire.

The silence broke open the stillness of the air in a sudden explosion, evaporating the street behind them.

She missed. They landed in the underpass instead. Something cracked.

Sparks ignited overhead.

Her mother lowered her to the wet cobblestone. They were running again, using whatever strength one good foot could provide across cinders that painted the walls above.

The mumbling returned. Her mother pressed her lips hard enough to draw blood until she swallowed the sound back into her throat.

Then they dove into the river. The water drowned the last of her mother’s voice.

The current hit her legs first.

A fourth Beat, stronger now.

The black water. Cold at her waist. Her mother’s arms — deliberate now in a way they had never been. They were all a step of defiance.

Her mother’s strength became a precise dance as she treaded carefully beneath the bridge, her body shaking against the current.

In perfect focus, her mother pushed further.

At the far bank above, a caravan waited on the horizon, signaling in prismatic lights she could not read and voices she could not understand.

Hands from the cart reached down through the rain-dark light.

Her mother stopped directly beneath the stone arch.

The embrace on her hand tightened. Then flashes of people moving — an arm, voices, unfamiliar hands reaching down.

Her mother did not release her.

A fifth Beat, filled with Dissonance.

In that instant, the weight was still warm. Her mother’s breathing came loud and ragged, the last anchor against the invasive, low-buzzing static that began its electrical hum again.

Those arms pressed her close one final time.

Everything about that hold was wrong. Too tight. Too brief. The last.

Then her mother pulled back. Just far enough to look at her. That face was ruined by blood, fever, the crossing — and something worse than all of it. Something that made every hair crawl along her fragile arms.

Then lifted her upward toward the light.

Her eyes, vibrant in resonance, did not close. They held — they held — until the arms on the cart lifted the remaining distance.

Her mother’s gaze became the last fixed point in a world coming apart. She does not remember letting go. Even now, she doesn’t.

But then she was atop the bridge road, carried by strangers who frantically sprinted away from her mother.

She tried to go back, turning around to reach her. It didn’t matter.

The Great Plague had no mercy for orphans.

The strange hands dragged her into the cart. Then the wheels kept moving, and far below, the dark water swallowed the bank.

She had one last sight of her mother in the rising sound of static.

A sixth, failing Beat.

Her mother called out to her, uttering one word.

Her mouth was already giving way to the violent, deafening flash when she said it.

Not a goodbye. Not an instruction. Not even a prayer. Just one word that carried the weight of all three.

She looked at her once. Just once. And said—

### Act 1: The Void in the Crown of Dying Static (Chapter 1)

_Movement 1: The Dream | Score I: A Ballad Before Iridia_
_Act 1: The Void in the Crown of Dying Static_

_Resonance | Key of Attunement_

“Amadea,” her mother had said.

The name that would become a crown, the weight that she would spend the rest of her life trying to deserve.

Four syllables and a cruel joke abandoned to a world of dying static; whether it was a command or hope, it didn’t matter. The silence opened before either.

A sudden cascade of explosions flared right where her mother should have been.

The cinders shook at the tail of the cart. Then light — too much light — and the memory suddenly stopped to nothing but the ringing sound in her ears.

For a fractured moment, the blinding light etched the shadow of a reaching arm into the wet cobblestone outside as a dark, permanent stain on the ruined bank.

Are there even Beats anymore?

She couldn’t answer.

There should have been a seventh, but she couldn’t count anymore. The dissonance was still in her ears, distorting the edges of everything in white noise as she clutched her knees into herself.

The last grip of the warmth of her mother’s hand had gone cold in hers somewhere in the space between the moment she had turned to a cascade of light and the next moons she cannot account for.

She felt only old, cracked wood, until even her fingers became numb from clutching it. The violent flash kept warping all her senses. It consumed everything but a faint, persistent smell.

Sweetness: faint, intoxicating, obscene.

A single, pale ember drifted through the splintered slats of the cart, landing on her numb knuckle. It glowed a faint, sickly amber before dying into chalky dust, leaving behind only that heavy, floral rot.

It was the kind of smell found in wild meadows, evoking the impossible gardens of the amber fields somewhere far beyond home. This scent was linked only to the sound of her mother humming wordlessly, low and tuneless — a fragrance mirrored by the way people hum when they have forgotten they are making any sound at all.

It was never supposed to be the smell of the dying.

Never the smell of corpses that had learned to dance. Never the fragrance of bodies convulsing in a climbing euphoria, until the air itself went rigid with static, until the stillness bloomed into a bright, violent flash.

The smell always came first. Then the coughing. Then the dancing.

Then detonation.

And the smell was here again; it snapped her back to reality, for a moment. That same smell made all of the strangers frantic.

Someone shouted for something to move. Someone else shouted over it, and beneath both a third voice had stopped forming words altogether. Chaos, absolute, and desperate until three more lights broke at once along the ridge, amber, silver, crimson, close enough together that the sound arrived as a single massive burst instead of three — the same unison her mother had gone still in. All at once.

The second burst of light shifted all color to white, only deepening the dissonance plaguing Amadea's senses.

The cart lurched hard enough to throw her against the crate behind her. Somewhere close, a woman was still shouting the same name over and over, as if repetition alone could hold reality in place.

The sudden movement made her realize there were more children with her, or at least seemed to be until their faces blurred. A crying face beside her folded into another crying face, and she could not tell — did not try to tell — whether she was watching a single kid weep twice, or two children grieve as one, whether the sound belonged to a boy or a girl or simply to the cart itself; all the details faded.

The static sound was at its most overwhelming. Amadea let the faces, the fire, and the silhouettes all blend into a single shadow that dissolved into it.

It was a pure, consuming white fog until everything went blank.

The next time Amadea opened her eyes, she was in a different cart now.

That much she knew because the wood beneath her fingers was rougher, splintering against her nails with every rut the wheels made over the crystallized craters of the road. The smell of tallow and wet, freezing iron had replaced the smell of sweetness.

Through the gaps in the floorboards and slit of the windows, the charred, golden dust of the wasteland rushed by in a hypnotic blur, painting dead, gray grasses in a sickly, unnatural sheen over the landscape of black trees.

Yet the static was always underneath, and all of the voices had ceased entirely.

All was a bygone memory, filled with the rattling of light, only anchored to the weight in her hands.

Several moons passed this way, clouded, though she could not have told anyone how many.

Every so often she remembers crying, when the detonations were at their most intense. They never cared for her. They always came regardless, in the wake of the euphoric, dissonant screams of more shadows and the void they left behind.

The cascades of explosions turned bright and soundless in the deafening instant before the sound arrived. It was lightning — always silent until it wasn’t. Such silence bloomed so bright it became the noise that made her ears ring even more.

The shower of explosions was always stuttering bursts of deep yellow, mellow orange, and, at its worst, wounded crimson — always accompanied by the long, pale shimmer trailing each light down to falling, fading cinders.

But at some point even the cascades dissolved into humming static.

Amadea became still and quiet in all the ways it makes a grieving child useful. She ate when food reached her hands. She slept when the cart jolted enough. Until somewhere in all that gray repetition, a voice had started, and unlike everything else, it did not fade back into the noise.

It was a naive voice, a few moons older than her, two lunar cycles at most — a tone old enough to carry a knife, yet young enough to still believe that same rusted blade meant something.

It spoke as though the world might one day owe him an answer.

He talked — well, he had talked as she would later understand — since before she noticed him reminiscing about a family that was taken by the Great Plague. Perhaps once he mentioned a neighbor who could not feed him either. Perhaps he spoke of other things, too.

She could never recall the details; none of them had stayed. Only the shape of them remained.

For her, it was all static. The caravan. His voice. The Phases in-between. She had existed in them without thinking, without arriving anywhere. At some point, he had even stopped speaking of his past. She could not have said when.

But he wouldn’t stop talking. His voice was stubborn enough to shift to other things, as though reaching to pull her back from the cascades of the violent flashes.

“You’re thinking too loud,” he yelled at her one night, close to her ear.

It was somewhere in the mouth of an arch pass while the distant lights continued appearing in the valley below.

The basin was a jagged maw of shattered spires and collapsed bridges, standing as a graveyard of architecture swallowed by the pitch black, with only firelight over the rubble in stark, violent contrast.

“I’m not thinking,” she said.

“That’s the problem. You’re never thinking about anything!”

A Beat, for the first time in many moons.

Then another.

“You only ever freeze!” he continued, annoyed.

But she couldn’t answer him. Amadea didn’t know how to explain that thinking required having something to think about, and that her mind had been overrun by dissonance and static.

It, too, had become an ashen, barren place where all memories went to die.

The boy sat alongside her.

He stayed silent for a long time — long enough that she thought he had given up like everyone else. Perhaps he should have.

But he never did. His voice broke the silence and spoke again; it held a familiar, almost dangerous tone. Softer and just as intoxicating as the smell of the amber rot of flowers. It was a voice she had not heard since the weight of her mother’s hand went still: the tender voice of a consoling secret he hadn’t told anyone else.

“There is a place,” he said, “where the trees grow violet.”

It was almost dusk.

His face was half-lit by the faraway fires. She looked at him with the disbelief reserved for the delusion of fools and poets — and at the end of the world, she wasn’t sure which was worse.

“A Ballad,” he insisted. “From a singer who came through my village, eight moons before the Luminant Rot drove everyone insane! At the very least when no one was screaming about the light of the plague yet.”

“He was old, a little bent, walking with a curved back while playing a lute, missing a string. He couldn’t have cared less. He sang for three days straight without ever repeating himself. Most of it I’ve forgotten, but there was one verse, one verse I never could—”

He paused, looked distant, searching within the memory.

She almost said something. She didn’t.

“He sang about a tree with violet bark. A tree with silver-white branches that swayed lightly in the moonlight. A tree beneath which there was a garden where flowers could not die because the roots drank from a silver spring so clear it became liquid glass, until it pooled to a deep blue with golden motes that mirrored the sparkling stars of the cosmos.”

The boy’s voice fell to something almost reverent.

“He sang about the most beautiful place in all the lands.”

“Singers lie,” she finally interrupted him.

“Sometimes. But they also remember,” he replied.

His gaze turned and locked with hers.

Their eyes met for a moment akin to an eternity. A gentle, fleeting exchange, but in that sliver of time she saw something she had no name for — an ephemeral spark she would later learn to recognize as hope. The dangerous kind of hope.

All in a single look, time froze in faith, honesty, and even the warmth that would keep Amadea alive even when she felt she had no right to keep breathing.

“Someone saw it. Then someone else put it into a song. That means it exists,” he said as time resumed anew, grounding her in the present. “Singers remember!”

“Singers remember…”

She stood quiet at those words for a moment.

“Even so,” she said at last. “What does it matter?”

She opened her hand toward the valley below, gesturing at the fading crimson and the shimmers still drifting alone through the pitch black in snowflake patterns. “We’re here. They’re there.”

A Beat.

“Everything I knew or loved was there… too… once.”

The cold reached her jaw at the intonation of those words. Her face was wet.

She did not wipe those tears. She didn’t think to — they had arrived the way the cold always arrived: intrusive, from somewhere outside her. The tears were the weight that lived deep within her chest without ever asking.

“Is there any point in loving anything that can be taken?” she shouted to the void creeping at her chest in desperation.

The boy fell silent.

She let out a sharp gasp and turned to a low, defeatist voice anew.

“It doesn’t matter. Nothing matters…”

“No," he reclaimed his breath, defiant, “That’s where you’re wrong.”

Looking directly at her eyes, he caught her gaze that was turning inward again before she could curl into her knees. He held both of her shoulders firmly, and with a strong grip, he declared:

"It does matter because we’re going to find it!”

She almost laughed.

She was starving, orphaned twice over, and the boy beside her had lost his mind before she could make sense of her own. He had been taken by a singer’s delusions, a broken poem, and their fairytale Ballads.

But he wasn’t laughing.

“We’re going to find it,” he repeated. “And when we do, we’re going to build something there. A city. A home. A place where no one who ever had to run will have to run again.”

“That’s so stupid,” she said. Her voice came out flat.

Her tears kept flowing, quiet and uninvited, and she let them. There was no point in stopping what she hadn’t started.

“Probably,” he agreed, finally releasing her. “But it’s better than sitting here waiting to die.”

From a burnt, oversized coat, he drew a weathered leather pouch and tipped a seed into his palm. It was a hard, almost crystalline fragment that caught the ambient dying fires of the wasteland in sharp refractions of pale rose against his skin.

He held it up between them.

“I found this the night the singer came through,” he said. “Next to him.”

“I don’t know what tree it’s from. But I’m going to plant it.” He turned it over in his fingers. “When I find the Violet Grove.”

“I promise! Even if it means going to the end of the world!”

He held the colored seed as though it were the last treasure in a broken land. When he finished, he put it back in the pouch and returned the pouch to his coat.

A ridiculous performance, she thought.

And yet, from that single, delusional act of defiance, something moved in her. Something had pierced the static, and it was the first night Amadea felt anything other than the hollow cold clawing at where her heart should have been.

She had no name for it. She only knew it was undeniably hers.

It was a novel desire, a minuscule thing barely enough to hold. But the cold shifted slightly to make room for it, and that was enough.

She noticed, distantly, that her face had dried. Just as those tears had flowed on their own, they now left only the shadows of fading rivers beneath her crystalline eyes.

The light in the valley below had dimmed. Neither of them moved.

“What is your name?” he finally asked.

That question pierced her, entering clean and deep, like a blade finding a wound that had never fully closed.

For many moons, she had lived as if she had none. Just “girl,” “orphan,” “child,” “you there,” but never a question beyond that. No one had asked. So her name had remained the only word she had kept for herself — silent, held in the dusk, as a distant memory for when she needed it most.

Amid the quiet after he asked, her memory stirred.

Not clearly, not all at once. Only a voice in the fever-dark — her mother’s voice, still alive, saying her name as though it stood as both prayer and command, before the weight holding her hand at the river crossing became absence.

She considered it now, the weight of it — and found the word already at the edge of her mouth before she had finished deciding.

She was willing to change the fate of the story.

“Amadea,” she said, and the word felt older than every ruin around them.

The boy went still.

A Beat.

“I — I know that name,” he mumbled, fiddling with his fingers.

“Was it…? Yes, it must have been one of the Old Ballads…”

“Yes! I remember!” The brightness came back at once. “A queen, or an empress — the titles change depending on who’s telling it. But it’s always the same legend. A regal woman who kept a kingdom alive and breathing long enough for it to become something real—”

“That’s not me,” she frowned, interrupting him.

“Not yet,” he replied, shifting to a reverent tone.

“Ah. At last, Empress Amadea.”

“I am Sir Artus.”

“And I promise you! One day, I will be the greatest knight you have ever known.”

She stared at him: at his proud grin, at the heroic pose he had struck as if he were the king of his own invented delusion, kneeling on a Ballad before a queen.

She didn’t know whether to be baffled, moved, or something in between, nameless.

Why had her name affected him so deeply? She couldn’t understand it. It was irrational. How could this boy — Artus — look at her pitiful state in the tattered clothes, a dying mind, and act as if she had emerged from a madman’s poem and forgotten it all?

But his absurd sincerity made her smile, for the first time in longer than she could measure.

She pulled her knees to her chest and looked back at the distant valley.

Artus turned, mirroring her movements on the tail of the caravan, settling next to her.

From the distance, a loud sound arrived.

He followed her stare as she was caught in something like wonder. If not for the destruction that every detonation meant, it could almost be called beautiful.

The cascades of light that had taken everything began again. 

One by one they all began with a whistle.

_Boom_

The first detonation opened at the low center of the valley in a deep amber crown. Wide, rhythmic, slow. Its petals unfurled in outward twinkles of light in a perfect ring before the whole shape shuddered and shed itself downward in long, pale threads that dangled in the dark like the memory it represented.

Away from the ground turned to cinders, the lights were comforting in their low, resonant thud, reaching her chest before her ears. A sound that came late and soft, almost gentle.

Almost.

_Boom_

Then another. Pale gold, with a tighter crown — a sudden, sharp star of light which blossomed in geometric patterns until it became a sphere. It held its shape longer than the first, every filament distinct, ordered, radiating clean and bright before the edges drifted into slow, glittering arcs.

The shockwaves moved through the bedrock, shivering up the iron wheels of the caravan and the coat draped over her knees.

She looked at Artus. His face was lit in amber, then gold, then rose — cycling with the valley. For a moment, looking at him, she saw the color of candle flame.

She felt something she had no better word for than warmth.

_Boom_

More lights rose from the ground all at once. Gold, cream, climbing together on their fire upward, their bursts colliding at the peak, gold threaded through pale white, the trails of each falling through the light of the others in crescent paths whistling in soft pops as they vanished.

The valley caught it all. What remained on the rooftops. The low line of the ridge. The road that led inward. All of it amber, then rose, then cold pale gold fading to crimson.

Artus drew a quiet breath.

“Static Criticality,” he said.

Then, low and almost to himself, he murmured a line — words she didn’t recognize, half-sung, half-said, the cadence of something elder than either of them, perhaps older than the refuges below that had become embers, whispers, and light.

It was certainly the fragment of another Ballad, its words worn flat from being remembered as many times as there were sparks on the ground below.

She didn’t ask which one.

The sounds of his voice came late, woven into the valley’s sounds. A soft percussion. A low thud. A dry crack. A long, resonant roll that faded before it finished.

They watched until the valley was almost quiet.

The caravan rocked gently on the road. Her shoulder knocked against his on a deep rut. Neither of them adjusted. His silhouette passed through the dark of another archway. A tight silver burst scattered into a hundred individual sparks, each trailing its own brief comet-tail halfway through the air.

Before the end of the tunnel, one light became wide amber again, its crown so broad it tilted against the dark above, long pale threads drifting down through the silver still falling beneath. Both lights landed together on the ruins below, overlapping what once had been walls.

“Do you think anyone is still down there?” she finally broke the silence.

“No,” Artus declared. Then, lower in watery eyes: “I don’t think so.”

She nodded once. Said nothing. Watched a new pale thread dissolve before it reached the ground.

She pulled his coat over her knees and held it there.

Artus reached into his coat. She heard the small sound of the leather pouch — there, then gone — just the feel of it, his hand closing around it for a moment before he let it go.

The caravan wheel struck a stone, a single shudder through the wood, through her palms, up into her wrists.

She looked at Artus.

She thought about her mother saying her name in the fever-dark. The way it had sounded — both prayer and command, as if the syllables themselves were worth her last breath.

She had tried not to think about that memory for a long time.

Perhaps it was him. The seed. The half-remembered Ballad spoken in the air of a burning valley, offered as a prayer. The way he had called her _Empress_ without laughing, and meant it — or meant something close enough that the difference hadn’t mattered.

Perhaps it was only that someone had asked in a voice full of Consonance.

She watched the last colors in the valley. Pale, vivid red embers still drifted through it, whispering before they reached the ground in their soft popping sound.

Then that too folded inward.

The last of the crimson lights was going out. The valley below had settled into the same dark as every place reduced to a wasteland, where the static had once been deafeningly loud.

The wheel’s rut, the coat over her knees, Artus’ shoulder against hers, all dissolving into the same formless dark that swallowed everything eventually.

“Amadea…” she mumbled.

### Act 2. The First Birthday of a Starving Caravan (Chapters 2-4)

#### Chapter 2 — An Awakening from Grief-Static (Act 2 | Part I)

_Movement 1: The Dream | Score I: A Ballad Before Iridia_
_Act 2: The First Birthday of a Starving Caravan_

_Resonance | Key of Attunement_

_Tap._

A pause. She inhaled.

_Tap. Tap._

“One.”

The sound arrived before the thought did.

One?

She exhaled.

There was a tapping noise, accompanied by the rhythmic hum of a faint, smoky voice. Each breath, every so often, stretched long enough to pierce the silence.

“Two.”

_Click._

The sounds carrying the number were low, unhurried, aimed at no one in particular, and they had a ticking quality that wound up every so often.

“Three.”

_Tap._

The interior of the wagon was dark, built from black hardwood grown pale with age, showing at the corners. Arched windows were cut into its walls in tall, gothic frames that let the moonlight fall through in narrow, almost cathedral-like slants.

It smelled of tallow, rust, wood, and the sour breath of children too hungry to dream.

In the shadows near the back, there was the main window. Large, imposing, pressing against the light shaft seeping from it. A man sat with his knees drawn up, a silver metal plate strapped over his kneecaps.

He kept another pause long enough to breathe in. Then again, the rhythm.

“Four.”

_Tap. Click._

He was keeping a flowing cadence arranged as a count. He did so while signaling at every surface in the wagon, almost pointing at her too, every so often. His large back was cloaked as it rested against a crate of cracked pottery over the sounds of moving wheels.

Amadea had not noticed him before. Well, she had not noticed anything before, but something in her latched onto the count after her last memory with the boy, the seed, and the Ballad. The tapping metal sound latched onto the count as her hand found a set of tattered ropes she didn’t know were reaching for.

“Five.”

_Tap._

She then noticed an ornate ring next, because the counting rhythm had led her eyes there. It was the source of the tapping. It sat heavily on the middle finger of that man, accompanied by the movements of his index finger as he was doing the counting.

The ring had a dull green band, veined faintly and interlaced with threads of light. Its center held a bright orange gemstone, drenched at the edges in the color of coal whose embers hadn’t decided whether to die or catch. Each time his ringed finger struck the silver kneepad, the vein-work of the gemstone showed itself.

It was captivating; it pulsed with every tap, like something breathing inside the fading crystal in a soft yellow-cream light. And each pulse landed at the exact same place in her chest — a low, physical anchor of both light and sound, dragging her one Beat at a time back into a body she had half-forgotten was hers.

“Six.”

_Tap._

Over a brief glance, the man noticed her noticing, alongside the growing fixation of her curious gaze on his finger. He did not stop counting; instead, he raised his other hand — scarred, calloused, the knuckles cracked from cold, with the rest of his arm swallowed by the sleeve of the same long, weathered, jet-black leather coat that cloaked beneath his back.

His hand made a steady motion, preparing his thumb to reach his middle finger.

“Seven.”

_Click._

He snapped his fingers.

At that instant, the gem in his middle finger vibrated, once, twice, and a small flame kindled at the tip of his thumb. The spark was no larger than a struck matchhead, but it was undeniably real; its steady flame danced in the air, passing through it and outside the window. He then touched the flame to the stub of a candle in front of him, wedged into a knot of wood and wax.

The wagon’s interior bloomed gold.

Amadea’s eyes followed the sudden light. As it ignited, it gave off a strong scent of herbs and earth that mingled with the rust still hanging in the air.

Amadea sneezed.

The man saw her once, and after a soft chuckle, he lifted a second strange instrument from the inside of his coat — a plated circular device that reflected as if made of brass. It was likely once golden, but now its main part, a circular plate with curved sigils, had rusted, matching the color of everything else in the room.

He wedged it in his arm, swinging it faintly through a chain that lacked even more color than the rest of the device.

_Click. Tap._

“One.”

The counting began again, but now to the rhythm of the pendulum. As the numbers progressed, he angled the trinket toward the sliver of moonlight bleeding through the arched shutter slats of the window behind him.

A Beat.

_He heard the symphony of stars above, the Great Harmonic Loom.”_

The veil around the fire thickened with each verse, shifting slightly in its prismatic colors to the hum under Elaine’s breath. For a Beat, Amadea swore the light of the flames dimmed at the edges, as if the legend beyond the circle had taken a step in to join them.

“It was there, in that vast howling nothing,” Artus narrated, “that the boy first learned to listen truly. Not to me, nor to you. To the world, and its sacred geometry.” He tapped the side of his head, two fingers to his ear, then pressed his palm flat over his chest.

“He did not listen with these. To feel the world, it isn’t done with the body. Magic is felt only with whatever’s underneath.”

“His fortune turned,” Artus went on, “on a day that should never have belonged to fortune at all.” The lute-stick dropped to a near-whisper, letting the cello carry.

Corvin’s baseline slid into a minor chord, and as the bow pressed harder, Artus’ voice dropped, and the melody turned grim. Amadea felt her skin prickle at the sudden chromatic shift that echoed in the cinders:

_“An architect of claw, and mind too fierce to tame,_
_Was dragged before the whip, defiant to his name._

_His master saw a monster, passed the genius by,_
_And dressed up murder as a trial,_

_All the injustice just to sentence his will to die.”_

“Daedalus,” Artus narrated, letting the name sit heavy. “Half-man, half-beast. A Demi-Human slave with claws enough to gut anyone able to rival his intellect. He stood for no master, no prince, and no monster.”

“And no judges,” Kay added under his breath. “Don’t forget judges.”

Artus pointed the neck of his stick at him without breaking rhythm, and the song rose again, louder now:

_“But from the dark, fate itself changed_
_The storm of the winds had led him there._

_The orphan stepped, whom death had failed to break,_
_And shattered the tribunal with just one vow he’d make._

_Their cruel chains, courts, and laws were monsters to hunt._
_And from the wreck, it rose an indomitable trust.”_

He snapped his fingers once. “The whole trial shook at their own disgrace, and through Aurelian the chains forgot how to exist,” Artus said, punctuating the remark by snapping his prop stick in half and tossing it into the campfire.

“Together, they ran. And in the open air, the golden winds caught up to them once again, and on a desolate clearing, Daedalus gave the nameless boy the one identity that would outlive them both—”

“Aurelian,” some of the children said in chorus.

“The first voice of the Auric Order,” Artus harmonized.

After letting their air settle for a brief moment at the legend’s title, he continued. “It was there,” this time gentler, almost reverent for four full Beats, “that Aurelian looked inward and found Resonance waiting for him.”

Corvin’s bow slid into a new progression. The baseline climbed, a careful ascent, testing the weight of every step to reach enough tension for a resolution. Artus sang:

_“He struck a chord where nothing dared to sing,_
_The first to teach a dying world to ring._

_For Resonance was never just wind and might,_
_It was the shaping of the golden Auric Aria’s light!_

_Attuned, the heavens bent around a singular whole;_
_Aurelian had discovered that all of Spellweaving,_
_Is merely the cymatics of the soul.”_

At the word cymatics, Elaine’s hum shifted. Her hands changed their figures, tracing smaller shapes nearer the fire’s skin. The veil answered, taking on a faint, rippling texture alongside the seams of its delicate topology.

“Motif Awakening,” Artus explained, glancing at Amadea as if the term were a secret he wanted her to remember. “The first of its kind. The moment an orphaned boy became the very first of the legends of all time as a Mythical Virtuoso.”

The children closest to the fire leaned forward, the blankets slipping from their narrow shoulders. Even the boy with the wet cough had gone completely still, anchored by the weight of the story.

“Finally, you got that part right!” Kay called out, a proud grin breaking through his usual smirk. “You actually listened to me.”

Artus flashed Kay a brilliant, fleeting smile, but the bard’s focus had already shifted. He lowered his gaze from the smoke of the fire, looking down to where Amadea stood underneath the shadows of the rocky stage.

“And so,” Artus declared, his voice dropping its theatrical boom for something startlingly intimate. He lifted his free hand and pressed it flat over his heart. “The two of them swore something older than any promise ever made.”

He extended that same hand down toward her. His palm was open in fingers gently curved. He waited, letting the invitation to join the stage hang in the cold space between them.

Amadea stared at his outstretched hand. As she realized what it meant, a sudden rush of white noise flared in her ears — the familiar, defensive static of her own survival instincts.

Perhaps it was the heat, or that the hand looked identical to the one underneath the stone arch of the river, or something else entirely, but every reflex screamed at her to step back into the dark. She tightened her jaw, fully prepared to shake her head and let the moment wither.

But Artus severed the distance with a single word.

“Consonance,” he said softly, as he closed his eyes.

He left his hand suspended in the air, offering her a terrifying, naive span of completely blind trust. It became clear he wasn’t going to grab her. He was leaving the choice entirely up to her. It felt like the second secret of the night, laid bare as the climax, mirroring Corvin’s moving bow.

“A bond, the Strand,” Artus whispered, eyes still closed against the dark, “that holds even when the world doesn’t make sense, when Coherence doesn’t exist.”

The campfire popped. The cello breathed a low, waiting hum. The static in Amadea’s chest fought against the profound, terrifying light of his offer. She could stay as a shadow in the ruins, or she could step into the song.

A Beat.

Her fingers found his.

Amadea uncurled her fist and reached up. She gripped Artus’ palm firmly, actively anchoring herself as she climbed up beside him.

At the contact, a soft smile broke across Artus’ face. He opened his eyes, his grip steady and sure as he helped her onto the stone. Still holding her hand, he raised it upward. He let his voice fall into a triumphant, melodic whisper to deliver the final lyric:

_“Two outcasts in a ruined land, rejected and alone.”_

“If the world will not be kind,” he quoted, his gaze locked intensely with hers, “we will have to build our own.”

Corvin’s cello answered the vow with the last chord that swelled — deep, resonant, and almost hopeful. Almost.

Artus released her hand and looked at the campfire.

“That was the daybreak of Laureated Marble, for the Age of Renewal had begun for the Golden March, with a great empire built on the slabs of golden dust.”

He took a bow before the crowd.

Amadea felt a strange, profound quiet, as though the music pressed itself to the seams between her heart and a second pulse she hadn’t known she possessed.

She looked around. Everyone was watching them on the makeshift stage. Elaine had stopped her gestures; instead, both of her hands covered her mouth, her eyes vibrant and sparkling just over the white fur of her collar.

Artus paused there, perched on the rock, holding the silence just long enough for Corvin to slide into a final, lingering note, the bow hovering just above the strings.

The entire caravan watched raptly as Artus’ throat moved with a heavy swallow as he regained his upward stance. His chest rose and fell with the effort of returning from wherever the story had taken him.

“That,” he said, his voice dropping to a single rasp, “is where this Ballad ends tonight.”

A low murmur of disappointment hummed around the fire. One of the older girls in the crescent ocean of blankets pouted outright. “But you said there was more! What about the rest of the Virtuoso!?”

“Act I is enough for starving bellies,” Corvin cut in, finally opening his eyes. His tone was dry but not unkind. “You start promising Act II, they’ll expect desserts.”

“We don’t have flour for Act II,” Elaine added pragmatically, finally letting her hum and her hands rest. “Yet.”

The veil around the campfire thinned as her voice quieted, becoming faint gossamer filaments of silk rather than an array of visible prisms. Amadea felt the outside sounds creep a fraction closer, though they still seemed muffled, like footsteps heard through snow.

Elaine, noticing the faint curl of smoke from the small scorched bundle of herbs she had left too near the flame, clicked her tongue as she picked it up.

“That’s enough theater,” she playfully replied. “If I burn our entire stock of grass for the sake of ambiance, Corvin will put me on the ration board.”

Artus and Amadea stepped down from the stage. The stone was cold and rough beneath her boots, stark in contrast to the warmth that still hummed through her from the performance. She still feels the ghost of Artus’ grip on her hand.

“Thank you,” he whispered once to the air.

A Beat.

He then immediately went to the coughing boy, dropping to one knee to ask about his performance. Amadea overheard the start of it.

“Did you hear the part where he sensed the Leylines pulse?” Artus asked in a conspiratorial voice. “I’m certain I botched the cadence there. Please be true — did it ruin the performance?”

Amadea stared at the dirt, letting the quiet wash over her. The soil was packed hard, worn smooth by the patches of dry grasses. She found herself tracing the patterns of the tiny pebbles that rocked against her feet.

Her mind was still echoing with the Ballad’s final phrase.

She looked toward Elaine, who was now recovering the heavy pot over the iron cradle of the campfire.

It hung over the mound, having cooked throughout the entire Ballad. Elaine was pouring in more water from a dented canteen and tipping a careful handful of earth-beans into it. The thin smell of something like food edged into the air, fighting with rust and tallow.

“Kay,” she called without looking up, “stop looming, the Ballad’s over, and bring the stores. If we’re going to pretend this is a feast afterward, I at least want everyone to have the same lie in their bowl.”

“On it,” Kay replied, swiftly disappearing toward the second cart of the caravan. He came back from the ramp with a sack that clinked and rustled, his expression caught between weariness and its quiet humor.

Amadea watched them move with the wordless economy of people who had repeated this page of their lives too many times.

Elaine’s hands were precise as she stirred the mixture together, almost dance-like, though she would have denied it if asked. Kay was nearby as he set the sack down and began to distribute small portions of hard bread, or at least what passed for bread, each piece as dark and dense that it might have just been river stone.

As they continued serving the rations of food over stale water, Corvin took another task.

He had a ledger of parchment and a stub of charcoal, beginning the arithmetic that went with the tapping rhythm of his frantic counting against the metal of the kneepads again.

Corvin was writing on a makeshift page, mumbling to himself how many beans, how many mouths, how many days between this moon and the next settlement.

His lips were moving constantly, and, between numbers, he kept repeating his time-tracking measures. “Well, as the Phase of Twilight ends, the Phase of Prelude follows by the next moon. The death of winter in the Echo of Silence is almost done, and the land will finally bloom in the three Phases of the Echo of Resonance. We’ll hopefully be in a better position if, of course, the stars aren’t lying again.”

He kept sighing to himself, drawing maps, entirely absorbed in the ticking of his celestial astrolabe.

Elaine then began handing the bowls to the children, a soup that smelled just good enough to be edible. The first were to the children nearest to the campfire.

And after the desperate satiated themselves, Elaine reached for Amadea.

The bowl was sturdy, unlike the caravan’s hardwood, yet still looked repurposed, as if it had once belonged to another object.

Amadea gave it a sip.

The liquid was warm against her lips, but the taste was entirely horrendous. It was bitter and earthy, and it felt hard to pass down because the aftertaste was the absolute worst part.

“Yeah, it’s an acquired taste,” Elaine smirked as she noticed Amadea with her tongue out. “I promise we’ll soon get something better, but we don’t have much to get by for now.”

She then adjusted her winged hat, moved slightly, postured, looked twice around them, and moved to an angle where the rocks would mask their shared silhouette.

“Okay, here,” Elaine whispered, tilting her head. “The performers of today’s show get an extra. But only today!”

Elaine then grabbed a small vial of a clean, silvery metallic liquid from the inside of her coat. She gently poured a bit into the mix, its texture like water but slightly viscous, oddly sparkling as it touched the soup’s surface, spreading in a slow, hypnotic pattern until it dissolved.

“Don’t tell anyone,” she winked. “It’s our little secret. I have very few of these.”

She then quickly left Amadea with the chipped bowl in both hands and moved elsewhere in the caravan, blending into the shadows of the firelight.

Amadea stared at the soup, the warmth seeping into her fingers, slightly unsure whether to take another sip. She was hungry, but the aftertaste was terrible — though as time passed, its smoke started to gain a faint sweet scent.

She gave Elaine’s strange liquid the benefit of the doubt and gave it another sip.

A Beat.

Her eyes opened. It was not the same flavor. The bitterness washed away entirely, or at least enough to make it truly edible. The gritty texture remained the same, but each chew was now oddly comforting, and the taste brought back slivers of memories of a gentler past.

The sensation passed as quickly as it had come, leaving her blinking with damp eyes; she quickly wiped them away.

Amadea stood alone on the rocks, watching the rest of the kids attempt to eat, and fail, until they gulped the broth anyway before the cold could take more of them.

Everyone ate in silence that was not quite empty — it remained filled with the memory of Artus’ Ballad of Aurelian, the Mythical Virtuoso of Resonance, and the low, steady ghost of Corvin’s baseline.

She kept trying to overhear the conversation between Artus and his attempt to cheer up the coughing boy, but she couldn’t quite make out the words. As it dragged on, Amadea didn’t hear the rest of it — another smell reached her before.

Something gone from a herb-sharp aroma to acrid, curling up from the fire’s edge. Elaine had left another pouch too close to the flame while her attention drifted elsewhere.

The sack was smoking with a grey thread rising from its scorched surface, and Corvin was still too absorbed by his own Beat-count focus to realize. She picked it up, and once her own plate was empty, she scurried past the children, making her way to the caravans beyond the campfire to follow after Elaine.

***

It didn’t take long; she found the glow of her hat a short distance off.

Elaine was not tending any of the herbs in the sacks by the caravan’s ramp at all, either. The little scorched bundles sat, forgotten and faintly smoking, on a flat stone.

The walls of the cart loomed above them; they were larger than Amadea had realized from the campfire. She had seen it from a distance, of course, but up close it was something else. They were not carts, but behemoths disguised as a fortress on hardwood wheels.

The main body was imposing above the ground. Its dark planks, scarred by a hundred repairs and stitched with iron bands and stretched leather, rose two full stories into a stage-like upper deck, its beams raked just enough to catch a performer in torchlight from below. The wheels alone stood taller than Kay or Corvin, iron-banded and vast enough to cradle a water barrel that Amadea could have hidden inside, with chain-rings bolted along their rims.

It wasn’t a wagon. It was a moving city divided into twin carts, each a fortress prepared for war.

But the caravan wasn’t what stole her breath.

In front of the great wheels, between the giant wooden spokes, Amadea finally saw what was pulling the carts.

It was dozens of enormous beasts, bigger than the wheels and each roughly twice the size of Corvin. Their soft, white fur gleamed with an azure glimmer, swirling across their hides like the ripples of a slow river.

They possessed broad, calm faces and long, tufted ears that flickered at the sounds of the night. Their horns, though seemingly small at first glance, curled across the clouds of their fur into the patterns of the stars above, faintly emitting the seeping moonlight, almost like the brass artifact of Corvin.

As Amadea stepped closer, one turned its massive head. Its eye — huge, glossy, and dark — blinked deliberately before returning to its hay.

This animal had certainly seen stranger things than a girl staring at it in wonder, Amadea thought to herself.

As she approached the front, she finally caught a clear look at Elaine. The white fluff on her coat suddenly made sense; it was woven from the shed fur of these very beasts. Kay stood beside her; the pelt on his coat was much darker, but it shared the same thick texture.

Elaine removed her hat. She rested a bare hand against a buffalo’s flank, her fingers moving in slow, rhythmic circles against the white pelt as she fed it herbs.

“Do you remember?” Elaine asked softly, her gaze fixed on the animal’s fur. “How we even got the original two out of that Domestication Enclave?”

The animal leaned into her touch.

“I remember you almost losing an arm,” Kay replied, his velvety voice dry.

“I remember you almost losing your dignity.” Elaine conjured a faint smile. “Strange, thinking of those Cycles as distant now. Before the second wave of the Luminant Rot — what were we?”

“Hopeful, still kind perhaps,” Kay said.

“Heyy! I’m nowhere near Corvin-level yet. What are you going on about?”

She drew her hat between them, its yellow-cream gem veined with teal green hues. “Look — First Ornament only! You haven’t even earned your second element.”

“So you’re still immature at heart,” Kay agreed.

She dropped the hat to her lap, her fist rising, ready to hit him.

“But!” Kay defended himself, raising an index finger to halt her.

“That’s the same light I saw in you that made any of this possible. They told us not even those decrepit elders could tame the Mist Buffalo. That they’d rather die than pull a cart.”

He paused, his brow furrowing as he chased a memory.

“What was his name? Sir something? No, it was Ber— something. Ah, it’s on the tip of my tongue.”

“Beryl, the legendary hunter of the Boreal Reach,” Elaine said, her gaze lowering to the grass.

Kay offered the smooth flourish of his hand to catch her eyes. His prismatic pink gem pulsed faintly in time with his breathing.

 “Exactly! See, that’s the Elaine of the singers that scout ahead. She always remembers.”

A faint, reminiscent smile touched her lips.

Kay leaned in, his velvety voice softening. “And yet here they are. I wouldn’t have believed you or those childhood dreams either, but you truly did make these Pure Light creatures docile. Sixteen of them now, and every single one chooses to stay.”

Elaine’s raised fist relaxed. “One of them chose to kick you in the ribs, if I recall correctly,” she murmured, the fight leaving her.

“That one was just establishing boundaries. I respect it.”

Kay caught her descending hand, lacing his fingers through hers, his smile widening for the length of a Beat.

“Now that the Prelude’s coming again, we can stabilize,” Elaine whispered. “Feed them properly. Rest them.”

“If Corvin doesn’t work us all into the ground counting stars first,” Kay joked.

She huffed something close to a laugh — as close and real as either of them had strength left for.

Amadea stood at the edge of their intimacy.

The burnt herbs in one hand, Artus’ unfinished Ballad a ghost behind her. She was caught between two things she didn’t fully understand: a boy performing defiance against a cruel world for her and a nameless coughing boy, and two adults who had clearly already paid that price themselves.

The smell of the herbs — now thoroughly scorched and giving off a charred bitterness — caught the attention of Elaine, who turned sharply.

She spotted Amadea standing in the shadows. Surprise crossed her face, then recognition, then something close to what Amadea had felt when Artus named her before the Ballad.

Elaine squeezed Kay’s hand hard enough to make him wince, and instantly put her feathered cap back on.

“Ah.” She crossed the distance, boots silent on packed earth. “Haha, I wondered where that went,” she said with an awkward laugh, taking the ruined pouch with a rueful shake of her head. “Well, that’s a loss. Thankfully, we have more.”

Her eyes met Amadea’s.

“So, you were watching the Azure Mist Buffalo.”

It wasn’t a question. Amadea nodded anyway.

“They’re beautiful. Stubborn. And the reason we’re still alive.” Elaine’s voice softened, its practical edge gone. “Pure Light creatures — you’ll have noticed the magical glow in their horns, the spirals in their fur.”

She stroked the flank of the nearest, right between its ear and its neck. The animal made guttural sounds, low, almost a song.

“This one’s Starlight. One of the first we had.”

Amadea only stared, heavy-eyed.

A Beat.

“Yeah, I know,” Elaine said before she could answer, her gaze dropping to the patches of dead grass. “Not the most impressive name. But it’s mine and one of the first I thought of.”

Amadea looked at her, which prompted Elaine to continue patting the animal until it made the sound again.

“And it’s the one she chose too, so that’s all that matters!” Elaine declared, half-proudly, in a wavering tone.

She paused, a strange expression crossing her face — half memory, half something else. “Remember the Ballad? About Aurelian building his empire from golden dust?”

Amadea nodded, only watching her silently.

“Well, that’s what we’re doing.” Elaine’s hand rose without thinking, brushing a stray strand from Amadea’s face. “Building something from nothing. Cold, and hard — We’ve got nothing to spare except hope and buffalo fur.”

Her smile thinned with use.

Yet a fierce, distant light caught in her eyes on a gaze drifting past the distant silhouettes of the dead trees, over into the pitch-black horizon.

“We are going north,” Elaine whispered, carrying a prayer. “To the place where the soil meets the moon.”

The gem on her cap flickered twice.

“The Avaloch basin,” Kay added softly from the shadows. He stepped closer, his velvety voice dropping raw, wrapping the air in quiet warmth.

“Hidden somewhere behind a mountain that dreams in fractals, sheltered by twin waterfalls, cloaked in silver sand.”

He glanced down at the prismatic pink gem on his gauntlet.

“It’s Elaine’s dream — and our purpose, kid. A sacred basin in the shape of a chalice. The one place where the symphonic veins of the world converge to keep the static criticality out, forever.”

Elaine stroked Starlight’s massive flank, her fingers trembling slightly against the white fur.

“To the rest of the world, it’s just a myth, Amadea,” Elaine replied, trying to lock her gaze with hers.

“But for me, for Kay, for the buffalo... and perhaps for Corvin too, this is a boundary built by the Aria herself. That is what we called the last procession.”

Amadea only blinked, the heavy, unreadable weight enough to crush Elaine's smile.

“I— I know how it sounds,” Elaine’s breath hitched, her voice withering like the popping cinders nearby.

“Yes, it could all be just a dream. But we’re here, building it anyway...”

Amadea tried to hold her gaze, wanting to understand Elaine's voice, but her eyes were losing the fight to stay open. The fire, the soup, and the warmth of the animals had settled into her bones at last. Her eyelids kept drooping despite her best efforts.

“Ah,” Elaine exhaled, the sound catching on a sudden wave of relief. “So that was it.”

Her shoulders slumped, letting out a soft chuckle over the weight of the breath she had been holding.

She then exchanged a look with Kay, who helped carry Amadea up into the coach box of the fortress-cart before slipping back toward the fire’s silhouettes.

Amadea curled against Elaine’s side near the buffalo pack, the animals’ slow breathing a kind of lullaby neither of them needed to ask for.

The warmth reached her in slow layers; first her hands, then her face, and finally something deeper. Corvin, Kay, Elaine — the adults who now carried names as much as Artus. Half-dreaming, she thought that somewhere far beyond the campfire, a nameless boy in a cruel Expanse had made a promise on a night like this to his Demi-Human friend, to build an empire from dust.

She wondered, as sleep finally claimed her, if that empire had looked anything like this. That dream — its consonance — was the key that attuned the song of her soul.

It was beautiful, and ephemeral.

The feeling didn’t last long; a coughing noise woke her before the light did.

It came from close by, shallow and wet, each breath sounding like it had to be excavated rather than drawn. Elaine slept on, exhausted past the reach of the sound. But Amadea’s eyes opened, and she saw silhouettes at a distance.

Across the dying coals of the campfire, she saw Corvin again. He was sitting with the coughing boy, his voice pitched too low for her to catch the words.

The boy’s shadow grew smaller against Corvin’s arm. It wasn’t shrinking in physical size, but consumed from within, much in the way the wax of the candle Corvin had lit in the caravan interior grew smaller as it burned.

Corvin murmured something to the little brass pendulum in his palm — a sound that might have been a prayer dressed as a habit — and then he wrapped his arms fully around the boy.

Amadea recognized the hold. It was the same wrongness. Too tight. Too brief.

When Corvin stood up, he carried the boy into the dark beyond the firelight.

He did not come back with him.

Amadea lay still at the edge of the fire, in between the carts of the caravan. She watched the space where their silhouettes had blended into the dark, feeling something in her chest tighten around the shape of a familiar absence. It felt like a profound void.

She never asked his name, and she hadn’t overheard it from Artus.

It was a name she could never recover.

When Corvin returned, alone, his figure moved more slowly than before, as though the walk back weighed more than the walk out.

He knelt by the shirt he’d left folded near the fire.

He drew a knife from his own coat and cut a small, careful square from the fabric. He worked with the exact precision he used to play his cello. Once it was severed, he took a single needle and sewed the linen patch into the lower half of his jet-black coat, with his fingers trembling only slightly against the firelight.

He looked up to the skies. Amadea looked up with him.

When she returned her gaze to the earth, Corvin lifted his ringed hand.

Where there had been nothing a moment before, the oak-worn cello now rested against his shoulder, condensing from a cloud of light.

Chimes echoed from nowhere once again, but this time, the sound didn’t disappear. The chimes deepened into soft, keyed notes, almost as if the skies themselves were singing as well.

Almost.

He drew the bow across the cello, accompanying the sound of the stars, and a low, mourning note rolled out into the dark of the night. It was a sound much different from the Ballad he had played — much darker.

Amadea stood mesmerized at the edge of the campfire. Corvin shifted his posture, keeping his eyes closed. There was no veil forming around the fire this time. The melody was the world’s resonance, mourning with him.

A Beat.

Then another.

And another.

As Corvin neared the bridge of the song, Amadea moved closer, drawn by the resonance of the cello vibrating through the entire caravan. As the performance continued, she sat by the cart’s wheel.

She stayed until the last note faded into obscurity, when the final breath of his song went silent.

In the aftermath, as the keyed notes finally faded, she stepped into the light and asked:

“Why the cloth?”

Corvin didn’t startle this time. He had felt her approach; a man like him always felt an approach.

“This linen,” he said, his gaze fixed on the strings, “is because someone should remember.”

She didn’t understand it fully, but the gravity of his words was enough. The nameless boy she would never see again had been given a shape — invisible, but real enough to keep.

“His soul now rests in the Eternal Symphony.”

Corvin let out a deep sigh, his back expanding on a deep inhale, offering another silent prayer as he looked at the stars.

“His is the note that echoes the melody of my instrument. If the keys of the Aria remember enough to play through this shared Resonance, we should too. It’s the last tether humanity has to the heavens.”

As the words settled between them, Corvin resumed his playing. It was another song, dancing with the cinders that moved to the tempo of the wind, accompanied by the voice of the Eternal Symphony echoing from nowhere.

Amadea kept quiet. She saw a mirror of the faces she remembered from when her mother ran with her. It was different, but the feeling was identical; Corvin played the same way her mother had carried her through the static criticality cascades.

When the memory blurred, or when the weight became too tangible, she left him to it and went to wake Artus.

She moved toward the third cart below, intuition guiding her through the dark to the last cart she hadn’t seen. Dawn was barely rising, faint sunlight seeping in rays through the branches of the tallest trees.

Amadea was halfway down the hillside steps when she heard dissonance. It was a low, wrong sound threading through the encroaching quiet. It filled the air like the chimes from before, but thin and rattling — like something dragged over broken glass.

A growl of pure hunger.

She turned.

It came out of the tree line, slow and stiff-legged. Another creature that looked like an animal. Nothing like the white buffalo. Wrong.

It had a long snout, its ribs showing through matted, patchy fur that almost melted away from its bones. Its ravenous eyes fixed on her with the flat, singular focus of a thing that had not eaten in longer than it could remember.

With the last fragments of its reasoning, it decided she would do.

It crept closer to the light. It was almost a wolf, but deeply disturbing in both flesh and sound. The core of its dissonance was a black sphere violently encrusted in the exposed ribs at the center of its chest.

Amadea’s pulse climbed to her throat before her mind could catch up to why.

She felt her mouth go dry as her hands turned to ice.

Then a second screech began underneath it — faint at first, then rising. That old, familiar sound bled back in: the static. The buzzing nothing that had swallowed her whole after the river. The white noise that distorted the edges of her vision. It flooded her ears just as the creature’s muscles coiled to lunge.

She couldn’t move.

Her eyes were transfixed in a tunnel vision that narrowed out the sight of everything but the fangs of the horror closing the distance. She couldn’t hear anything over the roar of her own unraveling mind.

The static clouded all her senses, as if it had never truly left, not even through the Ballad of the previous night.

She braced herself for the worst. Tears began to flow.

She closed her eyes.

A Beat.

_Thump._

A loud sound shattered the trance.

The creeping static broke open, but not into a violent, deafening flash.

Something crashed down from above her — a shape falling from the upper layer of the hill by the campfire, dropping with the devastating thud of a stone loosed from a sling.

It was Corvin.

Anchoring all of his weight on the cello’s endpin as it met the creature’s black ribs mid-leap with a sound like a heavy door slamming shut on a storm.

The two of them went down together in a tangle of claw and coat. In the same fluid motion, Corvin drove the lower spike of his cello — the very endpin that had grounded the Ballad earlier, sharpened like a weapon built as much for killing as for making music — straight through the beast’s chest and its black core.

As the cello pierced the sphere, the creature didn’t bleed. It let out a single, chilling scream.

Then it dissolved, unraveling into luminous motes that shifted between black and white, scattering on the wind like ash from an unlit fire.

And again.

The astrolabe and the moonlight had failed to place her, yet the bruises, the hunger, and the distant smoke had not.

The skies were silent — even if they were a beacon above — they never pretended otherwise.
