#### Medea, the Silver Blood, and the Haunting of the Violet Empress

**The Unseen Architecture: How Medea Prefigures Amadea

The revelation that Cordelia's aristocratic family descends from the fragmentation of the Silver Blood kingdom—and that Medea's death precipitated that fragmentation—is not mere background lore. It is **structural prophecy**. Medea's story is Amadea's story, played in a minor key, ended in tragedy. The Waltz of Wandering Love, composed by Medea at the founding of her kingdom, is the *ghost note* that has been playing beneath Amadea and Cordelia's relationship from the beginning, unrecognized, unfelt, but acoustically present.

When the revelation finally lands—in the chapters of the courts of delicacies and owls opposing each other—it does not merely inform. It **shatters**. For Cordelia, it is a spiraling moment because she realizes that the family that betrayed her, the family whose blood runs in her veins, is *complicit* in the destruction of the very lineage that produced the song she has been playing. For Amadea, it is a devastating recognition: the Violet Empress—the legendary figure she has been unconsciously modeling herself after, the archetype of the sovereign who rose from loss—*never existed*. Or rather, she existed, but not as the legend tells it. Medea was not a triumphant empress. She was a woman who was betrayed, stabbed thirty-three times, left to die on the steps of her own throne, crawling toward a silver tree that could not save her.

The name "Amadea" itself—the name Artus heard in a ballad and gave to her—is a **mythic drift** of Medea's story, filtered through generations of retelling, stripped of its tragedy, polished into a fantasy of untroubled sovereignty. Amadea has been chasing a ghost who never was.

**Part I: The Silver Blood and the Fracture of Legacy

**Medea's Kingdom: A Foundation Built on Fragile Silver

The Silver Blood kingdom was not a golden age. It was a **wound made polity**—founded by Medea after the devastation of the Demi-Human wars (in the Old Testament timeline) or after some earlier catastrophe (in the New Testament). The "silver blood" itself was the mark of Selenea's blessing—a direct lineage of Pure Light that gave the ruling family heightened resonance, longer life, and a deep connection to the Lunehymn that flowed through the Leylines.

But the kingdom fell. Medea was betrayed—stabbed thirty-three times, according to the lore, by those closest to her, including [[Junius]], one of her most important confidants. She did not die immediately. She crawled to the top of her silver throne, but fell midway. The cinematic sequence of her death shows her imagining a peaceful scene: her confidants by her side, the dying tree gleaming blissfully in moonlight. The final shot transitions from her daydreaming escapist fantasy to the reality: she is alone, dying on a pool of silver blood in her ruined cathedral, having failed to crawl the steps of her throne.

**The tragedy of Medea:** She was not defeated by an external enemy. She was betrayed by her own blood, by those she trusted. Her death was not heroic sacrifice; it was *abandonment*. She died imagining a family that was not there, a peace that had never been.

**Cordelia's Family: The Inheritors of Betrayal

Cordelia's aristocratic family are not the Silver Bloods themselves—they are the **fragments** that survived the collapse, the nobles who either participated in the betrayal or stood by while it happened, and then rebuilt their power on the ruins of Medea's dream. They are the *inheritors of complicity*.

**The hidden guilt:** Cordelia does not know this. Not consciously. But the Submissive trait she carries—the readiness to be punished, the expectation that her worth is contingent on enduring suffering, the belief that love must be earned through pain—is the *psychological residue* of her family's original sin. They betrayed Medea, and then they betrayed Cordelia. The pattern repeats across generations: the family destroys what it claims to love.

**The fragmentation as original wound:** The Silver Blood kingdom did not fall because of external invasion. It fell because those who were closest to power turned on each other. The fragmentation is not just political; it is *relational*. Cordelia's family history is not a story of glory lost; it is a story of *trust broken*.

**Artus's Mythic Drift: The Ballad That Became a Lie

Artus heard a ballad about "Amadea"—the empress from the Silver Blood kingdom, the one who kept her people alive, the one who was crowned in a field of violet flowers. But that ballad was a **mythic drift** of Medea's story, filtered through centuries of retelling, stripped of its horror, polished into a fantasy of untroubled sovereignty.

**What Artus believed:** He believed in a Violet Empress who was triumphant, who built a kingdom from nothing, who was crowned in beauty and ruled in wisdom. He gave that name to Amadea, and he gave her the seed, and he told her she would be that empress.

**What was actually true:** Medea was betrayed, stabbed, left to die alone on the steps of her throne. Her kingdom fell not because she was weak, but because she trusted the wrong people. She died not in glory, but in a pool of her own silver blood, imagining a peace that never came.

**The cruel irony:** Artus's gift—the name "Amadea"—was a promise of a future that had already failed. The empress he believed in never existed except as a fantasy constructed from the ruins of a real woman's tragedy.

**Part II: The Waltz of Wandering Love as Haunted Song

**Medea's Composition: A Love That Could Not Find Its Home

The Waltz of Wandering Love was composed by Medea at the founding of the Silver Blood kingdom. It is, ostensibly, a love song—a melody about a love that tries to find love, that wanders through the world seeking its other half, that never quite arrives but never stops searching.

**The hidden meaning:** But the song was written by a woman who would later be betrayed and left to die. The "wandering love" is not just romantic longing; it is the love of a queen for her kingdom, for her family, for the people she trusted—love that was not returned, love that was answered with knives. The song's underlying somber undertones are not decoration; they are the *truth* of the composition, buried beneath the surface melody.

**The transmission to Cordelia:** Cordelia learned the Waltz from her family—not as a historical artifact, but as a *lullaby*, a traditional tune passed down through generations. She does not know its origin. She does not know it is Medea's song. She plays it every day because it is the only song she knows that makes her feel less alone. But the song carries Medea's grief in its structure, Medea's betrayal in its harmonic shifts, Medea's death in its unresolved cadences.

**The transmission to Amadea:** Cordelia plays the Waltz to Amadea in the wilderness clearing. Amadea hears it. She does not know its origin either. But the song *works*—it heals her, because the emotional authenticity of Cordelia's performance carries the accumulated weight of Medea's original grief, layered through generations of Silver Blood survivors, now offered freely to someone who is also grieving, also wandering, also looking for a love that will not betray her.

**The Spiraling Moment: When the Truth Lands

In the chapters of the courts of delicacies and owls opposing each other—when the [[Court of Delicacies]] (the Loss-Beauty Lunatics who followed [[Junius]] after Medea's death) confront the Court of Owls (Amadea's shadow order)—the truth of the Waltz's origin will be revealed.

**For Cordelia:** This is a spiraling moment. She has been playing a song composed by the woman her family betrayed. The song that has been her comfort, her identity, her connection to Amadea—was written by the *victim* of her own bloodline. She has been carrying Medea's grief without knowing it, playing Medea's longing as if it were her own, and in a sense it *is* her own—because the family that betrayed Medea also betrayed Cordelia. The pattern is the same. The song is the witness.

**For Amadea:** This is a devastating recognition. The Violet Empress—the figure she has been unconsciously modeling herself after, the archetype Artus gave her—never existed. What existed was Medea: a woman who loved, who trusted, who was stabbed thirty-three times, who died alone on the steps of her throne, who crawled toward a dying tree that could not save her. The legend is a lie. The hope Artus planted was built on a myth that erased the tragedy at its core.

**The realization:** "The Violet Empress never existed." The woman who came before ended in tragedy. There is no unbroken lineage of sovereign power. There is only Medea, bleeding out, alone, imagining a peace that was not there.

**Part III: The [[Court of Delicacies]] — Loss-Beauty Lunacy

**[[Junius]] and the Followers: The Aestheticization of Grief

[[Junius]] was one of Medea's most important confidants—the one who betrayed her, who stabbed her, who left her to die. After Medea's death, [[Junius]] and his followers did not simply move on. They *aestheticized* the tragedy.

**Loss-Beauty Lunatic:** This is the evolution of those who followed [[Junius]]. They do not grieve Medea; they *worship* the image of her death. The silver blood, the dying tree, the moonlit throne, the final cinematic of her imagining peace—these become *artifacts* of a beauty that can only exist through loss. They wear masks not to hide their faces, but to *become* the masks—to perform the tragedy, to live inside the aesthetic of betrayal, to turn Medea's suffering into a *style*.

**The [[Court of Delicacies]]:** This is the organization that has been haunting Cordelia and Amadea since the pugilist attack. [[Kastor]], the fox-masked pugilist is one of them. They are not merely assassins; they are *theologians of loss*. They believe that beauty is only real when it is destroyed, that love is only pure when it ends in betrayal, that the highest form of devotion is to *preserve the moment of breaking*.

**The contrast with the Court of Owls:** Amadea's shadow order operates on the opposite principle: "We die in the dark so that you may live in the light." The Court of Owls does not aestheticize loss; they *transform* it. They do not worship the moment of breaking; they use it as fuel to protect others from breaking. The [[Court of Delicacies]] preserves the wound; the Court of Owls heals the wound—or at least, prevents it from being inflicted on others.

**The Mirror Between Courts

| [[Court of Delicacies]]                 | Court of Owls                                                           |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Founded by [[Junius]] (the betrayer)    | Founded by Amadea (the betrayed)                                        |
| Loss-Beauty Lunatic: loss as aesthetic  | Loss as transformation: "To have lost greatly is to have loved greatly" |
| Preserves the moment of breaking        | Prevents breaking from happening to others                              |
| Worships Medea's death                  | Honors Medea's life (eventually, after the revelation)                  |
| Masks as identity (perform the tragedy) | Shadows as protection (die in the dark)                                 |
| The past as eternal wound               | The past as foundation for the future                                   |

The [[Court of Delicacies]] is what happens when grief is not witnessed, when betrayal is not healed, when the only response to loss is to *enshrine* it. The Court of Owls is what happens when grief is transformed into service, when the witness refuses to look away, when the only response to loss is to *prevent* it from happening to someone else.

**Amadea's Shattering on the [[Weight of Purpose]] and Rebuilding

**"The Violet Empress Never Existed"

This realization—arriving in the confrontation between the courts—is Amadea's second death of Artus. The first was his physical death. This is the death of the *fantasy* he gave her: the dream of a pure, unbroken lineage of violet sovereigns, the hope that she could become the empress of a legend that had never been true.

**The crisis of identity:** If Medea was not the triumphant Violet Empress, then what is Amadea building toward? If the legend is a lie, then is Iridia also a lie? If the name "Amadea" was given to her based on a mythic drift of a tragedy, then who is she really?

**The answer that emerges:** The legend was a lie, but the *song* was true. Medea did compose the Waltz of Wandering Love. Medea did love, did trust, did hope. She was betrayed, but the love was real. The tragedy does not erase the love. It *includes* it.

**Artus's Mythic Drift as Gift and Curse

Artus heard a ballad that had drifted from Medea's story, and he believed it. He gave Amadea the name, the seed, the promise. He did not know he was passing on a myth. He believed—with all his heart—that the Violet Empress was real, that the violet grove existed, that the future could be built on the foundation of that legend.

**The gift:** Artus gave Amadea a *direction*. Without the legend, she might never have left the caravan, never have picked up the violin, never have survived the death of her mother. The myth was a *necessary fiction*—a star to navigate by, even if the star was not actually a star but a reflection of a reflection.

**The curse:** The myth also blinded her. She has been chasing an ideal of untroubled sovereignty that never existed, believing that if she could just reach the violet grove, everything would be resolved. The revelation that the violet grove is not a place—that Medea died alone, that the silver blood kingdom fell, that the legend is a lie *and always was*—is the breaking of the [[Celestial Astrolabe]]. She must learn to navigate without the star.

**The Reconstruction: Iridia as Honest Foundation

What rises from this shattering is not a continuation of the myth, but a **new foundation**—one built not on the fantasy of an untroubled past, but on the *recognition* that all founding is flawed, all myth is drift, all legends are filtered through loss.

**Amadea's new understanding:** She does not need to be the Violet Empress. The Violet Empress never existed. What exists is Medea's love, Medea's betrayal, Medea's death—and Cordelia's playing of the Waltz, and Artus's belief, and her own stubborn refusal to stop moving forward even when the ground collapses beneath her.

**The name "Amadea" reclaimed:** The name is no longer a promise from Artus about a future that was never real. It is a *responsibility* to the truth—to remember Medea not as a legend, but as a woman who loved and was betrayed, who died alone, whose song became a comfort to a girl whose family had betrayed her, whose tragedy became the foundation of a city built on the principle that *no one should have to bleed alone*.

**The Echo Across Generations

The Medea-Silver Blood backstory creates a **three-generation resonance**:

And:

| Second | Artus | Hears the ballad, believes the myth | Passes the name to Amadea |
| Third | Amadea | Lives the name, builds Iridia | Discovers the myth was a lie, builds anyway |

**The Waltz as Strand: Echoing Bonds Across Time

The Waltz of Wandering Love is not just a song. It is a **Strand anchor**—a memory made audible, a bond woven across generations. Medea composed it; it passed through Cordelia's family (as a lullaby, as a tradition, as an unconscious inheritance); Cordelia played it to Amadea; Amadea will pass it to Iridia.

**The Echoing Bond:** Medea and Cordelia never met. Medea and Amadea never met. But the song connects them—not because of blood, but because of *shared wound*. Medea was betrayed by those she trusted; Cordelia was betrayed by her family; Amadea lost Artus, the only person who ever believed in her. The Waltz is the frequency that resonates through all three.

**The revelation's impact:** When Cordelia learns that the Waltz was Medea's composition, she is not learning *new* information about a historical figure. She is learning that her own grief—the grief she has been playing every day, the grief that connected her to Amadea—has a *history*. She is part of a lineage that she never chose, a lineage of pain and love that stretches back to a woman who died on the steps of her throne. The spiraling is not just shock; it is *recognition*. She has been singing Medea's story without knowing it. She has been Medea, in a sense—loving, trusting, being betrayed—and the song has been carrying her through it.

**The [[Court of Delicacies]] as Dark Mirror of Iridia

The [[Court of Delicacies]] is what Iridia could have become if Amadea had surrendered to loss rather than transforming it. They worship the moment of Medea's death; Amadea builds a city in the name of preventing such deaths. They wear masks to become the tragedy; Amadea's owls work in the shadows to ensure others do not have to carry the same weight.

**The final contrast:** The [[Court of Delicacies]] preserves the wound. The Court of Owls *heals*—not by erasing the wound, but by turning it into a *shield* for others. The Loss-Beauty Lunatics say: "Loss is beautiful. We will keep it forever." Amadea says: "Loss is transformation. We will build something from it."

**Conclusion — The Haunted Song and the Honest City

The revelation of Medea's true story, the origin of the Waltz, and the nature of the [[Court of Delicacies]] does not destroy Amadea. It **rebuilds** her—on a foundation that is not the fantasy of an untroubled past, but the *truth* of a tragic one.

**For Cordelia:** She learns that her family's betrayal of her is not an isolated cruelty but a *pattern*—the same pattern that destroyed Medea. She also learns that the song she has been playing is not just her own grief; it is Medea's grief, and Selenea's grief (through the Silver Blood), and the grief of everyone who has ever loved and been betrayed. She is not alone in her wound. The Waltz is the proof.

**For Amadea:** She learns that the name Artus gave her was built on a myth—but the myth was not *empty*. It was a mythic drift of a real woman's life, a real woman's love, a real woman's death. The legend was a lie, but the *truth* beneath the legend—Medea's courage, Medea's love, Medea's refusal to stop trusting even when trust killed her—is the foundation on which Iridia can be built.

**For the novel:** The Medea backstory transforms the Waltz of Wandering Love from a simple healing song into a **haunted chord**—a melody that carries the weight of betrayal, the ache of abandonment, and the stubborn, irrational persistence of love that keeps loving even after it has been stabbed thirty-three times and left to die. That is the song Cordelia plays. That is the song Amadea hears. That is the song that becomes the anthem of Iridia—not a fantasy of untroubled sovereignty, but a hymn to the ones who keep loving anyway, who keep building anyway, who keep playing the song even after they learn that the Violet Empress never existed.

*The city is built not on a legend, but on a grave. The song is played not in triumph, but in memory. The name is carried not as a promise, but as a question: What do we owe the dead who dreamed of a future they never saw?*

*Amadea's answer: We build it anyway. We plant the seed. We play the Waltz. We keep wandering, keep loving, keep searching for a love that may never find us—because the wandering itself is the song, and the song is the only thing that makes the silence bearable.*

**[[Medea]] and the [[Waltz of Wandering Love]]**

The first masked operative the Court of Owls detains in this chapter is not a fighter. _"We have been following a song,"_ the chronicler says.

_"What song."_

He reaches into the satchel — slowly, because Amadea's hand is on her saber — and removes a single folded sheet. He sets it on the table between them and smooths it flat with both palms. It is a transcription. The key signature, the waltz time, the particular ornamental phrasing in the third measure.

Cordelia, standing at the window, turns.

The chronicler is watching her, not Amadea.

_"You know this one,"_ he says. Not a question.

Cordelia's hand tightens on the sill. The transcription on the table is not exactly the version she plays. The notation is older, more formal, stripped of the small variations she has accumulated over years of playing it alone in the estate's back corridors where no one came to correct her. But the structure is the same. The wandering motif in the melody. The unresolved cadence in the fourth bar that she always played through rather than correcting, because correcting it felt wrong in a way she could not name.

_"Where did you get this,"_ she says.

The chronicler smooths the sheet again, a gesture that is almost affectionate. _"Where did you?"_

He does not perform the revelation. He delivers it the way an archivist delivers a document: matter-of-factly, without decoration, as if the weight of the information is entirely the listener's problem.

The Waltz of Wandering Love was composed at the founding of the Silver Blood kingdom. The composer was their queen — Medea of the Silver Blood, first sovereign of the Selenea-blessed lineage, whose founding of the kingdom is the reason the Lunehymn concentrations in that territory ran deeper than anywhere else in the Old Testament age. She wrote it at the moment of the kingdom's establishment, not as a celebration but as a reckoning — with the wandering she had done before the founding, the love she had carried through it, the people she had hoped would find her once she had built something permanent enough to be found.

_"A love trying to find love,"_ Cordelia says, almost to herself.

_"That is what she called it, yes."_

There is a silence in the room that has a different quality than the silences that preceded it.

Amadea is very still at the table. She is looking at the transcription, not at the chronicler, and her expression has not changed — but her left hand has moved from the saber hilt to the surface of the table, flat, and her fingers are pressing against the wood the way they press against the neck of the violin when she is working through a new sequence: small deliberate pressure, not playing, just feeling.

_"She's been dead for a long time,"_ Amadea says. Her voice is neutral in the particular way that means she is doing arithmetic. _"How does a founding song end up as a lullaby in an aristocratic estate."_

The chronicler looks at Cordelia.

Cordelia already knows the answer before he speaks. She has known something adjacent to it for years without allowing herself to approach it directly — the way she knew the shape of the Waltz before she knew its name, playing it by ear from the fragment she first heard from her grandmother, who heard it from her grandmother before that, handed down through generations of the family as _traditional music_, as _old custom_, as _something we have always played_, without origin and without explanation.

The Silver Blood kingdom did not simply fall. It was dismantled from the inside. Medea was betrayed by her closest confidants — stabbed thirty-three times in her throne room — and the aristocratic class that survived the collapse, the noble families who had either participated or stood by or simply moved quickly enough to claim territory in the aftermath — those families rebuilt their power on the architecture of what she had built, using the same Leyline concentrations, the same civic structures, the same cultural inheritance, without acknowledging where it came from.

Cordelia's family is not the Silver Blood. They are the **inheritance of the betrayal.**

What she is feeling is not grief, exactly. Grief she knows the shape of. This is something older and more structural — the sensation of a floor revealing itself to have been built over a body. Not metaphorically. Literally. The family that broke her for giving freely, that punished her for warmth, that handed her the whip she was meant to offer to the person beating her and taught her to say _thank you_ afterward — that family had been playing the song of the woman they destroyed as a _lullaby_.

She had been playing it in defiance of them, in the back corridors, the only music she kept that was only hers.

It had never been only hers.

It had always been Medea's.

Here is the structure of Cordelia's spiral: it does not arrive as a breakdown. It arrives as a series of recognitions, each one landing before she has finished processing the previous one, each one drawing the same shape at a different scale.

The song she played to resist the estate was the song of the woman the estate's founding betrayed.

The song she played to heal Amadea in the wilderness clearing was a founding song — composed at the moment when someone else had also tried to build something permanent enough to be found.

The song whose title she engraved on the linen tissue — the tissue Amadea used to find her in the dark after the crystal awakening, the tissue that sent Amadea on the rescue mission through the estate — was a three-hundred-year-old piece of evidence, a ghost that had been trying to name itself in her hands every day since she was a child and she had not known what it was testifying to.

She has been Medea. Not in the grand sense. In the granular one: loving freely inside a structure designed to punish the act of loving. Playing the song of someone who also loved freely and was stabbed for it. Carrying the grief of a woman she never knew, encoded in harmonic structure, without knowing whose grief it was.

The unresolved cadence in the fourth bar that she never corrected — because correcting it felt wrong.

It is wrong. It is unresolved because Medea died before she could resolve it. She composed the song at the founding of the kingdom and she never heard how it was supposed to end because the kingdom ended first, and she ended with it, alone on the steps of her throne in a pool of silver blood, imagining a peace that was not there.

Cordelia's hands are shaking. She becomes aware of this as a piece of external information — her hands are shaking against the window sill — and she tightens them into stillness with the deliberate practice of someone who has been trained to accept pain without visibly registering it. Then she stops. She lets her hands shake. She has bled enough. She is not performing composure for the estate anymore.

_"The song is the witness,"_ she says, and she is not talking to the chronicler.

Amadea hears her.

Amadea's Recognition — _The Violet Empress Never Existed_

It lands differently for Amadea.

She has been doing the arithmetic since the chronicler said _Medea of the Silver Blood._ Eight years ago, she was a girl sitting in a ruined arch-pass while a boy named Artus told her about a ballad he had heard — a story about a queen, a grove, an empire built in violet flowers. He gave her the name from that ballad. He planted the seed. He told her that she would be the empress of a legend that had already happened, that she was not building something new but fulfilling something ancient, that the violet grove was real and waiting.

He was eight Moons from the Great Plague when he heard that ballad.

He misremembered the tree species. He misremembered the ending. He fabricated triumph where there was only betrayal — not out of dishonesty but out of the desperate alchemy of hope, the way grief converts its rawest material into fuel because the alternative is not surviving the winter.

The Violet Empress was Medea. The violet grove was a Glimmerfern grove. The empire that Artus believed was a triumphant precedent was a wound made polity, ended by a knife, ended by trusted hands.

Amadea at her Crystal Motif Awakening — on the ground, holding a linen tissue engraved with the title of a three-hundred-year-old founding song, with the Sky Glass gone and Cordelia gone and Artus already dead — had compressed every loss she had ever carried into a single wish: _I wish I had the power to transform all of this agonizing loss into anything that would, one day, make real the title of my greatest knight._

She had been wishing herself into a precedent that ended in tragedy.

She has heard Cordelia play this every day for months.

She has heard Cordelia play this on the night she was injured, bleeding in a wilderness clearing, barely conscious, and the song reached her before the healing did — reached her in the part of the brain that processes frequency before it processes language, before she could decide whether to accept comfort or refuse it.

She was already inside Medea's song before she knew Medea existed.

The World Shaping Maverick does not need a [[Celestial Astrolabe]]. The World Shaping Maverick is the premise that she _is_ north now. The Violet Empress never existed.

That is not the obstacle.

Cordelia plays it that night.

Not as healing. Not as texture of companionship. Not as the private resistance she has practiced in back corridors since childhood. The Waltz of Wandering Love was composed by a woman who loved, trusted, was betrayed, and died alone imagining a peace she never reached. The song survived. It passed through three hundred years of hands that didn't know whose grief they were carrying. It reached a girl in an aristocratic estate who played it as defiance. The Violet Empress never existed.

# Serialized Version of [[Amadea]]

#### Act 0: Prologue

The last thing her mother gave her was weight.

Not warmth. Warmth had been gone for two days by then.

No, never a gentle thing. What she had was the draining of light that follows when the last candle in a fleeing home finally burns out. Not the warmth of the flame but what is left behind when everyone had escaped. It was the attuned, sacrificial, and absolute weight of the shadow it left. 

A haunting silhouette in the space behind.

That was the only inheritance left in her arms, the [[Void]] of a hand that had stopped answering, no matter how hard she clutched it.

She remembers the third river crossing — or at least a fragment of it.

Her mother carried her through desolate streets marked by screams. Mumbling something, desperate, followed by the somber sound of euphoria she kept biting back.

An explosion evaporated the street behind them. Her mother leaped to the lower pass toward the bridge, barely dodging a hurled fist of fire.

They landed. Something cracked.

Sparks ignited overhead. Her mother ran again with her, using whatever strength one good foot could provide across cinders that painted the wall above.

The mumbling returned. Her mother pressed her lips hard enough to draw blood and swallowed the sound back into her throat.

Then they dove into the river. The water drowned the last of her mother's voice.

The black water. Cold at her waist. Her mother's arms — deliberate now in a way they had never been. They were all a step of defiance, a precise dance as she treaded carefully beneath the bridge, her body shaking against the current.

Her mother pushed further.

At the far bank above, a caravan waited on the horizon, signaling in lights she could not read and voices she could not understand.

The embrace tightened. Then people moving — an arm, voices, unfamiliar hands reaching down.

Her mother stopped directly beneath the stone arch.

She did not release her. She pressed her close one final time. In that instant, the weight was still warmth. Her mother's breathing came loud and ragged, the last anchor against the invasive static that began its low electrical hum.

Everything about that hold was wrong. Too tight. Too brief. The last.

Then her mother pulled back. Just far enough to look at her. Her face was ruined by blood, fever, the crossing — and something worse than all of it. Something that made the hairs on her arms crawl.

She lifted her upward toward the light.

Her eyes did not close. They held — they held — until the arms on the cart lifted her the remaining distance. Her mother's gaze became the last fixed point in a world coming apart.

She does not remember letting go. Even now, she doesn't.

But then she was atop the bridge road, carried by strangers who ran away from her mother.

She reached back. They dragged her into the cart. The wheels kept moving. Far below, the dark water swallowed the bank.

She had one last sight of her mother calling out for her.

The [[Great Plague]] had no mercy for orphans.

Her mother uttered something before she ignited in the rising electric static that took the air in a violent, deafening flash.

Not a goodbye. Not an instruction. Not even a prayer. Just one word that carried the weight of all three.

She looked at her once and said—

## [[Amadea]]: [[Sonata]] of the Violet Empress

### Score I. A [[Ballad]] Before [[Iridia]]

#### Act 1. The [[Void]] of the Crown of Dying Static

“[[Amadea]],” her mother had said.

The last grip of the warmth of her hand went cold in hers somewhere in the space between the moment she was lifted and the next [[Moon]]s she cannot account for.

At some point the weight was in her arms, and then there was only the river’s cold in the scream of fires, opening in an explosion that reached the back of the caravan where her mother should have been.

The cinders flared at the tail of the cart. Then light — too much light — and the memory stopped to nothing but the ringing.

But even then, she recognized what the smell meant.

The sweetness always came first: faint, intoxicating, obscene. It was the kind of smell found in wild meadows, evoking the impossible gardens of the amber fields somewhere far beyond home. This scent was linked only to the sound of her mother humming wordlessly, low and tuneless—a fragrance mirrored by the way people hum when they have forgotten they are making any sound at all.

It was never supposed to be the smell of the dying.

Never the smell of corpses that had learned to dance. Never the fragrance of bodies convulsing in a climbing euphoria, until the air itself went rigid with static, until the stillness bloomed into a bright, violent flash.

The smell always came first. Then the coughing. Then the dancing.

Then detonation.

All of it was a bygone memory, filled with static and the rattling of light. Beautiful and deadly patterns carved themselves into the sky, ending in the strange, belated understanding that she, too, had weight when the wavering embers fell.

She always cried, but the detonations never cared. They came regardless, in the wake of the euphoric, dissonant screams. The cascades turned bright and soundless in the deafening instant before the sound arrived. It was lightning — always silent until it wasn’t. Such silence bloomed so bright it became the noise that made the ears ring.

It was a shower of explosions, [[Static Criticality]] blooming across the low valleys in stuttering bursts of deep yellow, mellow orange, and, at its worst, wounded crimson — always accompanied by the long, pale shimmer trailing each light down to falling, fading cinders.

They always happened, whether she wept or not.

[[Amadea]] had to learn that long before she learned what her own name meant — long before she could imagine that names could carry any meaning at all.

For her, words were just the sounds she made to keep the dark from pressing too close when the sparks ignited overhead. Allegedly, a name was far more than that. She had only ever learned through the body, first, in hazy, insistent memory. Her trembling hands already knew what her mind had not yet been asked to hold.

The caravans had formed as an act of the desperate and the still-living, when the frenzy consumed every settlement, when the infected started to dance until their hearts burst and, in the [[Void]] the explosion left behind, collapsed from within — until something like lightning struck them and turned them into the next cascade of lights.

Those who remained had to organize themselves against the encroaching static the only way they knew how: they moved, carrying the fool’s hope of reaching somewhere still sane while bearing the ghosts of everyone who couldn’t.

These wagons carried whatever could be carried. Food that would not last. Weapons that would never be enough. And children — dozens of them, gathered from villages turned to embers. From the sides of roads where their families had stopped moving. Picked up from doorsteps where someone with a failing, still-beating heart had left them before walking back into the dark to die alone, rather than pass the sickness on.

[[Amadea]] was one of those children. She was small enough to be forgotten, and quiet enough that forgetting came easily.

The caravan she joined was moving east, away from the burning southern passes, away from the places where the skin on your arms crawled, and the hair rose before the silence broke open deafeningly. It was a cart moving desperately away from the [[Static Criticality]] cascades that had overrun the low valleys in the flashing wake of the [[Great Plague]]'s destruction.

It was not a merciful caravan, only a practical one. Not quite good people. Not quite cruel ones. Only survivors, and survival had already worn the edges off everything that wasn’t functional.

Only the dead had let their failing hearts beat loudly enough to detonate. They kept any children who could slip through gaps, haul scraps, and vanish into the smoke of crowded places, and in return asked for little. That was Amadea. She was useful in all the ways grief makes a child useful: silent, small, alert, and easy to overlook until she was needed.

But she wasn’t a ghost. She learned quickly — or at least learned to tell which ruins still held food and which held things that wore hunger as a face. She noticed the soil tremors that came first, always followed by a growing dread. One after the other, forever in that haunting order.

The dread did not follow the tremors. No — the tremors began because something nearby was feeding — [[Atonalis]], the adults called them.

They fed at dusk.

That was why dusk went silent. But silence, at least, did not break open. Only the loud static did.

Still, [[Amadea]] looked up, and the stars always remained. The stars — the [[Constellation]]s high above — were the only light that never went out. She had noticed, in the [[Moon]]s since the weight of her mother’s arms became still, that they burned brightest not at dusk but in the deepest hours, when the dark was at its most complete.

It was as if they needed the dark to mean anything truly beautiful. But [[Amadea]] felt how much less comforting they were when there was no longer a roof between her and the firmament. The stars are truly beautiful, but they serve only as beacons, never as a home. And the silent skies had never pretended otherwise.

And yet, within all the dire static — amid the silence before it, in the light when it came, and in the dusk, and in everything that moved through it — she had learned something else. There was a boy in the caravan who still spoke as though the world might one day owe him an answer.

He was naive, a few [[Moon]]s older than her, two [[Lunar Cycle]]s at most. Old enough to carry a knife. Young enough to still believe that same rusted knife meant something. He carried the broken blade like a symbol of strength, mud-stained, almost as if it were a noble’s sword obscured by soot.

He had sometimes spoken of his family — taken, he said, in the first wave of the [[Great Plague]]. Perhaps once he mentioned a neighbor who could not feed him either, before he joined the nomadic wagons. Perhaps he spoke of other things, too.

She could never recall the details; none of them had stayed. Only the shape of them remained.

For her, it was all static. The caravan. His voice. The [[Phase]]s in-between. She had existed in them without thinking. Without arriving anywhere. At some point, he had even stopped speaking of his past. She could not have said when.

But he wouldn’t stop talking. He shifted to other things, as though reaching to pull her back from the cascades of the violent flashes.

“You’re thinking too loud,” he told her one night, close to her ear.

It was somewhere in the mouth of an arch pass while the distant lights continued appearing in the valley below.

“I’m not thinking,” she said.

“That’s the problem. You’re never thinking about anything!” 

A [[Beat]].

“You only ever freeze!” he continued, annoyed.

But she couldn’t answer him. [[Amadea]] didn’t know how to explain that thinking required having something to think about, and that her mind had been overrun by static. It, too, had become an ashen, barren place where all memories went to die.

The boy sat alongside her.

He stayed silent for a long time — long enough that she thought he had given up like everyone else. Perhaps he should have.

But he never did. He broke the silence itself and spoke again, and this time his voice wasn’t static. It held a familiar, almost dangerous tone. Softer and just as intoxicating as the smell of amber flowers. It was a voice she had not heard since the weight of her mother’s arm became still: the tender voice of a consoling secret he hadn’t told anyone else.

“There is a place,” he said, “where the trees grow violet.”

His face was half-lit by the faraway fires. She looked at him with the particular disbelief reserved only for the delusions of fools and poets — and at the end of the world, she wasn’t sure which was worse.

“A [[Ballad]]” he insisted. “From a singer who came through my village, eight [[Moon]]s before the [[Great Plague]] drove everyone insane.”

“He was old, a little bent, walking with a curved back while playing a lute, missing a string. He couldn’t have cared less. He sang for three days straight without ever repeating himself. Most of it I’ve forgotten, but there was one verse, one verse I never could—”

He paused, look distant, searching the inside of the memory.

She almost said something. She didn’t.

“He sang about a tree with violet bark. A tree with silver-white branches that swayed lightly in the moonlight. A tree beneath which there was a garden where flowers could not die because the roots drank from a spring so clear it became liquid glass, mirroring the sparkling stars of the cosmos.”

The boy’s voice fell to something almost reverent.

“He sang about the most beautiful place in all the lands.”

“Singers lie,” she said.

“Sometimes. But they also remember,” he replied.

His gaze turned and locked with hers.

Their eyes met for a moment akin to an eternity. A gentle, fleeting exchange, but in that sliver of time she saw something she had no name for — an ephemeral spark she would later learn to recognize as hope. The dangerous kind of hope.

All in a single look, when time froze in a moment of faith, honesty, and even love, that would keep [[Amadea]] alive even when she felt she had no right to keep breathing.

“Someone saw it. Then someone else put it into a song. That means it exists,” he said as time resumed, grounding her back to the present. “Singers remember!”

“Singers remember…”

She stood quiet at those words for a moment.

“Even so,” she said at last. “What does it matter?”

She opened her hand toward the valley below, gesturing at the fading crimson and the shimmers still drifting alone through the pitch black in snowflake patterns. “We’re here. They’re there.”

A [[Beat]].

“Everything I knew or loved was there… too… once.”

The cold reached her jaw at the intonation of those words. Her face was wet.

She did not wipe those tears. She didn’t think to — they had arrived the way the cold always arrived: intrusive, from somewhere outside her. The tears were just the weight that lived deep within her chest without ever asking.

“It matters,” the boy said, defiant. “Because we’re going to find it.”

She almost laughed.

She was starving, orphaned twice over, and the boy beside her had lost his mind before she could make sense of her own. He had been taken by the delusions of a singer, a broken poem, and their fairytale [[Ballad]]s.

But he wasn’t laughing.

“We’re going to find it,” he repeated. “And when we do, we’re going to build something there. A city. A home. A place where no one who ever had to run will have to run again.”

“That’s so stupid,” she said. Her voice came out flat.

Her tears kept flowing, quiet and uninvited, and she let them. There was no point in stopping something she hadn’t started.

“Probably,” he agreed. “But it’s better than sitting here waiting to die.”

From a burnt, oversized coat, he drew a weathered leather pouch and tipped a seed into his palm. He held it up between them.

“I found this the night the singer came through,” he said. “Next to him.”

“I don’t know what tree it’s from. But I’m going to plant it.” He turned it over in his fingers. “When I find the violet grove.”

“I promise! Even if it means going to the end of the world!”

He held the colored seed as though it were the last treasure in a broken land. When he finished, he put it back in the pouch and the pouch back in his coat.

A ridiculous performance, she thought.

And yet, from that single, delusional act of defiance, something moved in her. Something had pierced through the static, and it was the first night [[Amadea]] felt anything other than the hollow cold clawing at where her heart should have been.

She had no name for it. She only knew it was undeniably hers.

It was a novel desire, a minuscule thing barely enough to hold. But the cold shifted slightly to make room for it, and that was enough.

She noticed, distantly, that her face had dried. Just as those tears had flowed on their own, they now left only the shadows of fading rivers beneath the contour of her crystalline eyes.

The light in the valley below had dimmed. Neither of them moved.

“What is your name?” he finally asked.

That question pierced her, entering clean and deep, like a blade finding a wound that had never fully closed.

For many [[Moon]]s she had lived as if she had none. Just “girl,” “orphan,” “child,” “you there,” but never a question beyond that. No one had asked. And so her name had remained the only word she had kept for herself — silent, held in the dusk, for when she needed it most.

Amid the quiet after he asked, her memory stirred.

Not clearly, not all at once. Only a voice in the fever-dark — her mother’s voice, still alive, saying her name as though it stood as both prayer and command, before the weight holding her hand at the river crossing became absence.

She considered it now, the weight of it — and found the word already at the edge of her mouth before she had finished deciding.

She was willing to change the fate of the story.

“[[Amadea]],” she said, and the word felt older than every ruin around them.

The boy went still.

“I — I know that name,” he mumbled, fiddling his fingers.

“Was it…? Yes, it must have been one of the old [[Ballad]]s…”

“Yes! I remember!” The brightness came back all at once. “A queen, or an empress — the titles change depending on who’s telling it. But it’s always the same [[Legend]]. A regal woman who kept a kingdom alive and breathing long enough for it to become something real—”

“That’s not me.” she frowned, interrupting him. 

“Not yet,” he replied, shifting to a reverent tone.

“Ah. At last, Empress [[Amadea]].”

“I am Sir [[Artus]].”

“And I promise you! One day, I will be the greatest knight you have ever known.”

She stared at him: at his proud grin, at the heroic pose he had struck as if he were the king of his own invented delusion, kneeling on a [[Ballad]] before a queen.

She didn’t know whether to be baffled, moved, or something in between, nameless.

Why had her name affected him so deeply? She couldn’t understand it. It was irrational. How could this boy — [[Artus]] — look at her pitiful state in the tattered clothes of a fleeing caravan and act as if she had emerged from a madman’s poem story and forgotten it all?

But his absurd sincerity made her smile, for the first time in longer than she could measure.

She pulled her knees to her chest and looked back at the distant valley.

[[Artus]] turned, mirroring her movements on the tail of the caravan, settling next to her.

He followed her stare as she was caught in something like wonder. If not for the destruction that every detonation meant, it could almost be called beautiful.

The cascades began again, one by one.

The first detonation opened at the low center of the valley in a deep amber crown. Wide, rhythmic, slow. Its petals unfurled in outward twinkles of light in a perfect ring before the whole shape shuddered and shed itself downward in long, pale threads that dangled in the dark like the memory it represented.

From a distance, the sound arrived.

Away from the ground turned to cinders, the lights were comforting in their low, resonant thud, reaching her chest before her ears. A sound that came late and soft, almost gentle.

Almost.

Then another. Pale gold, with a tighter crown — a sudden, sharp star of light which blossomed in geometric patterns and held its shape longer than the first, every filament distinct, ordered, radiating clean and bright before the edges drifted into slow, glittering arcs.

She looked at [[Artus]]. His face was lit in amber, then gold, then rose — cycling with the valley. For a moment, looking at him, she saw the color of candle flame.

She felt something she had no better word for than warmth.

Then, more lights rose from the ground all at once. Gold, cream, climbing together on the right, their bursts colliding at the peak, gold threaded through pale white, the trails of each falling through the light of the others in crescent paths.

The valley caught it all. What remained on the rooftops. The low line of the ridge. The road that led inward. All of it amber, then rose, then cold pale gold fading to crimson.

[[Artus]] drew a quiet breath.

Then, low and almost to himself, he murmured a line — words she didn’t recognize, half-sung, half-said, the cadence of something elder than either of them, perhaps older than the refuges that had become fire and light.

It was certainly the fragment of another [[Ballad]], its words worn flat from being remembered as many times as there were sparks on the ground below.

She didn’t ask which one.

The sounds of his voice came late, woven into the valley's sounds. A soft percussion. A low thud. A dry crack. A long, resonant roll that faded before it finished.

They watched until the valley was almost quiet.

The caravan rocked gently on the road. Her shoulder knocked against his on a deep rut. Neither of them adjusted. His silhouette passed through the dark of another archway. A tight silver burst scattered into a hundred individual sparks, each trailing its own brief comet-tail halfway through the air.

Before the end of the tunnel, one light became wide amber again, its crown so broad it tilted against the dark above, long pale threads drifting down through the silver still falling beneath. Both lights landed together on the ruins below, overlapping what once had been walls.

“Do you think anyone is still down there?” she finally broke the silence.

“No,” [[Artus]] said. Then, lower: “I don’t think so.”

She nodded once. Said nothing. Watched a new pale thread dissolve before it reached the ground.

She pulled his coat over her knees and held it there.

[[Artus]] reached into his coat. She heard the small sound of the leather pouch — there, then gone — just the feel of it, his hand closing around it for a moment before he let it go.

The caravan wheel struck a stone. A single shudder through the wood, through her palms, up into her wrists.

She looked at [[Artus]].

She thought about her mother saying her name in the fever-dark. The way it had sounded — both prayer and command, as if the syllables themselves were worth her last breath.

She had tried not to think about that memory for a long time.

Perhaps it was him. The seed. The half-remembered [[Ballad]] spoken at the air of a burning valley, offered as a prayer. The way he had called her *Empress* without laughing, and meant it — or meant something close enough that the difference hadn’t mattered.

Perhaps it was only that someone had asked.

She watched the last colors in the valley. Pale, vivid red embers still drifted through it, whispering before they reached the ground.

Then that too folded inward.

The last of the crimson lights was going out. The valley below had settled into the same dark as every place reduced to a wasteland, where the static had once been deafeningly loud.

“[[Amadea]]…” she mumbled.

#### Act 2. The Economy of a Starving Caravan

**Draft Piece**
Several [[Moon]]s passed.

The caravan had developed its own economy. Mercy was not the currency.

It fed itself the way all desperate things feed themselves — by taking what others had left behind and, sometimes, by taking taking what they still hadn't.

The adults handled the larger thefts: gathering dying livestock, stored grain, the hollowed contents of farmhouses whose owners were either dead or had fled far enough to never come back.

However, for the subtler kind of work, the kind that required someone who could slip through a half-window as smoke, or vanish behind the wooden frames of still functioning market stalls without drawing a second glance — they had the children.

And among the children, they had [[Artus]] and [[Amadea]].

They came to be a theatrical yet reliable tool for acquiring valuables. They were very competent at it. They had discovered what every other adult had forgotten: a story, well-told, was the golden key of manipulation.

Not speed. Not ruthlessness. Not strength. No, the secret was the flawless performance of a harmless story.

They acted on the [[Ballad]]s they knew, and improvised whenever they forgot the ending. [[Artus]] was the lead, he played the charming distraction — the boy who crossed the square with a loaf of stolen bread beneath one arm, the actor who could start a fight with three words, and a match beneath the other.

At times, he even became the ear to a despairing man's entire life story, by finding the nerve that hadn't yet healed.

He had a gift for being noticed in exactly the right way, for making himself the loudest thing in the room while the room was otherwise engaged. He was great at being the undeniable center of attention.

That's precisely why [[Amadea]] learned to weaponize his antics, in a different art entirely.

She played something harder — the elusive shadow that follows the light as sure as night follows the day. She was the puppeteer beneath the strings of what all eyes are tracking, the part of the performance that blends so thoroughly within the audience that it leaves no trace.

People looked at [[Artus]]. People looked through her.

She had learned, in the uncountable [[Moon]]s since her mother's death, to make herself into something that the eye slid past without catching. She was present, always, but just barely outside the edge of the main stage. She was absent in all the ways that mattered for each theft, with terrifying and reliable piercing precision.

Together they were the tide that washed through half-ruined towns. Him drawing every gaze wherever he needed it. Her dissolving into the spaces that the distraction opened. They [[Dance]]d in every play their own waltz of light and shadow — and as payment for the performance, they took what the world owed them: bread, hard cheese, dried meat, and, on their finest occasions, the vivid luxury of the vibrant pink of [[Auric Peach]]es.

[[Artus]] reached for any [[Auric Peach]] whenever the opportunity allowed. They were always worth the effort — dense with flavor and strangely filling in the way that a stale meal rarely was, their flesh mirroring the texture and substance of real meat. Their color shifting from deep rose to a hammered gold depending on how well they had ripened, as though they were remembering what warmth looked like.

They appeared in every [[Ballad]] [[Artus]] knew from before the [[Great Plague]], always as a symbol of abundance, and perhaps of a life that was equally as vibrant in its normalcy, where this was the kind of fruit that had once been commonplace enough to appear in the street stalls without wonder.

The best ones were always the golden ones. Their name said as much.

Though the old [[Legend]]s gave them a grander origin — gifts of a distant 'Golden Sovereign,' the one theology the adults still whispered about even now in silent prayer. The alleged auric voice that sang the universe into being, whose song had become, for reasons no one fully agreed on, inexplicably silent. Though [[Amadea]] had little patience for the theology of adults.

She revered the distant skies, yes. They were of a myriad symbols but only in the way that their starlight provides, only in the awe of what she could see. Still, even if it was all a [[Legend]], she understood what [[Artus]] knew without either of them needing to say it.

Finding an [[Auric Peach]] in a ruined market was a kind of proof. A proof that a world had existed before this one.

The proof that things had once grown golden, and full, and beautiful without being loud or deafening. It was a bridge between the world that came before them, and the one past the embers that they were still — stubbornly, absurdly — trying to reach.

Once, when they were scavenging for exactly that, they found something stranger still: a riverside workshop standing half-collapsed at the edge of a flooded town, its shelves lined with objects that had no names in their vocabulary — filled with all sorts of things that were fascinating in the way wrongness is fascinating. They found many things that drew the eye and held it slightly too long. Eerie aromatic masks with sealed chambers, tools whose purpose they couldn't guess but whose sharp shape implied one, and anything stranger wedged still between them.

In the upper shelves, they found what appeared to be an entire wheel of pale cheese. [[Artus]] reached it first, and declared victory before he had even tasted it. Only when he bit it did he realize it was wax.

But that never stopped him from declaring victory anyway. The wax would waterproof their boots, he said. And that was practically the same thing.

Some towns, however, were not always abandoned, and the people who had survived the [[Great Plague]] had survived it, in many cases, precisely because they were ruthless about protecting what remained.

The first time they were caught, [[Amadea]] was barely over 12.

The market town had looked quiet from the hill — quiet enough that they had grown careless in acting their play.

This one merchant was much faster than he looked. He was skeletal, but those old bones moved with much more purpose than either of them had expected, and he kept his vision on his troves regardless of [[Artus]]' performance. When he caught the sight of [[Amadea]], his grip became the kind that doesn't negotiate. The kind that caught her arm before she cleared the valuables in the stall. The kind that left bruises in her skin for much longer than she would encounter him.

What happened next, she would remember for the rest of her life.

[[Artus]] saw both, he dropped the performance. He leapt, impossibly, to close the distance.

He stepped between them, before the merchant's hand could cause her more harm.

Not with a weapon. Not with a threat. He stood only with himself — in the plain, deliberate fact of him, placing his body where hers had been, telling the merchant with pristine [[Composure]] that the girl had been mistaken. It was he who coerced her to take the bread, and that he was willing to accept whatever consequences it carried.

The merchant, denied the satisfaction of punishing someone helpless, accepted the substitution, on the premise that he would be ruthless.

He accepted.

No one else from the caravan intervened.

[[Artus]] took the beating with the same [[Composure]] he brought to everything — quietly, on his feet, as long as he could manage, without making a sound that may draw [[Amadea]] back. He only looked once to the alley where she ran, and the brief exhale of relief on his face when she was safe was the worst part.

[[Amadea]] didn't run far, she watched from the edge of the shadows of a doorway across the square, her stolen bread still in her arms. She could not move. She wasn't sure she was breathing. She only noticed until her fingers clawed at the bread from shock and shame.

When it all ended, he found her at the edge of town, sitting in a low wall with the bread on the ground beside her — as through she had set it down between breaths, and had forgotten it was there.

He came walking slightly off-center, favoring his left side. His lip was split at the corner, already beginning to swell.

He was grinning.

"You kept the bread!" He said.

"You're bleeding." She said.

"Well," He sat beside her, carefully, and looked out at the road. "That's what the bread was worth."

"It wasn't your fault. You didn't have to—"

"Yes, I did." No drama. No performance. Only his voice stating a truth he had already reconciled without her.

"Obviously, I did."

She didn't know what to say to that.

She had never had anyone stand between her and anything that wanted to hurt her. The [[Static Criticality]] had never cared if she was crying — the cascades came whether she wept or not, indifferent as wounded weather, indifferent as the cold in a dead woman's arms.

But [[Artus]], that young boy from the caravan, who carried no obligation to her — none she had given him, none she could name — had walked into an adult's anger, and absorbed it on her behalf, as though it were simply the only reasonable thing to do. He walked as though the logic of it were obvious. He had done it without hesitation, without an audience, and without a single soul from the caravan moving in to help either of them.

That was the part that settled in her. Not the act itself. The solitude of it.

He was looking at her, in the way he always looked at the seed in his coat pocket — as if the future the stars pointed toward were already decided, and the present pain was simply the distance between the origin and the [[Legend]]. As if being hurt were something the story had already accounted for.

That rational, irrational gesture, was the first miracle she had ever witnessed — not because something impossible had happened, but because something she had no prior category for had happened instead. Not silence. Not relief. Something more precise. And it was him, it was only ever him, that quiet, and impossible him. It equalized her.

The static of her heart didn't go quiet. No — the frequencies shifted, the way a signal reshapes when something finally enters the register it had been solely broadcasting into the dark alone. For the first time, the weight of her mother's arms had something answering it. Something in the same key.

But even if his sacrifice moved her, it would not be the last time.

Over the years, in the half-alive towns they passed through, it became a pattern: [[Artus]] framing on himself the consequences of whenever they were caught, stepping forward whenever [[Amadea]] drew the wrong attention from the wrong adult. It was not often but it was often enough.

He never made it feel like a sacrifice. He was very tactful about that — the way he cared to choose his words after, the way he never let the cost show on his face until she had already looked away. He never let it settle as debt.

There was a fiery focus to the way he moved forward, not performance, but the kind of defiance that burns in the place where a person has already decided that the outcome doesn't change what they do.

He stepped forward each time with the same completeness he brought to every [[Stubborn]], irreversible act of resistance against the static of the world. The same completeness he had brought to sitting beside her in the mouth of an arch pass, speaking into a valley going dark at the edges, its embers still drifting — as though even the dying light owed him an answer.

And each time he stepped forward, the thing she would spend years struggling to name grew slightly heavier in her chest.

Until the weight became the story.

#### Act 3. Remnants of Hope and [[Ballad]]s

**Draft Piece of the Laureated Cart**

[[Artus]] went quiet when he saw it.

That was how she knew it was real.

She had been sitting in the inner fold of the caravan when she noticed he had stopped talking. She looked up. He was facing forward, held by something, with the same arrested stillness and quiet wonder he only ever had when he thought no one was watching him turn the seed over in his fingers, as though checking it was still there.

She rose to follow his gaze.

The wheels hit stone before she had fully stood.

The jolt was small but wrong — not the familiar pull of uneven pockets of packed dirt and gravel, but something harder, flatter, a surface that rang low and rhythmic under the cart, returning the weight rather than swallowing it.

She caught the frame to steady herself. The sound came a half-second after the feeling, steady and deliberate. Intention, she thought. Not desperation.

The caravan had reached a [[Developing Town]] larger than most of the [[Outpost]]s and ruins it moved through, and [[Amadea]] felt the difference before she fully understood where she was.

She pulled herself upright and looked out alongside [[Artus]], the wheels still moving beneath them.

Her first impression of the place was beauty. She had never seen a walled settlement. From the road it announced itself not with noise but with presence — the many shadows of it cast long against the pale sky, silhouettes of stone that multiplied as the caravan drew closer, each one more deliberate than the last. 

What struck her was not just the size of it, but the intention in every line, from the walls that rose at right angles to the towers that answered each other across the distance. The stone had been placed to mean something and had gone on meaning it long enough to answer everything the world had tried.

Then they passed through the gates, and she saw it more clearly.

The walls were rough-hewn, visibly weathered by time. Mortar patched in mismatched colors where sections had been rebuilt after one too many things had tried and failed to break them. The gates bore scorch marks the stonework had not bothered to conceal, in patterns that had the same stuttering geometry as the cascades she knew from the valleys.

Whatever had burned against them, had not gotten through, and it was clear the populace had chosen to remember that. It was as if the very erosion had happened through will alone.

It was one of the walled settlements that had truly survived the [[Great Plague]] at its most devastating [[Moon]]s by sheer ruthlessness.

Inside, it had the particular density of a place that had learned to want nothing it couldn't protect, but to fiercely guard whatever remnants it did have. The market quarter was real — stalls with actual roofing, merchants with actual ledgers, guards stationed not for ceremony but for memory. 

Everything for sale was precisely weighted. Nothing was freely given. Even the noise of it was different than the loose, desperate noise of caravan camps: this was the sound of commerce between people who intended to be alive next [[Cycle]], conducting business accordingly to the future.

And if this place had really survived, then it had earned something equally real: guards who remembered why they stood there.

[[Amadea]] had felt it the moment they passed through the gates. In the way attention here moved differently — less dispersed, more deliberate, focused. There were many eyes that had practice deciding what did and did not belong, and had learned across too many bad seasons to assume the answer was neither.

The caravan was assessed in the first seconds it entered, then labelled an outsider.

[[Artus]] had felt it too, and gone quieter than usual.

Until he saw something.

He didn't tell [[Amadea]] what. Only that it was worth the run. She had learned by then to trust his instincts the way she trusted the dread in the air before the [[Atonalis]] hunger leaped from the edges of the dark at dusk — not because it was comfortable, but because it was reliable. He was never reckless without reason.

But this time the reason was hidden from her.

That should have been her warning.

It was a traveling merchant's cart near the rear of the quarter, half-canopied in cloth trimmed in gold-veined white geometry that mirrored the forests. [[Artus]] instantly recognized the colors, it was the signature of what he had heard in [[Ballad]]s.

"Laureated marble." He said to himself in amazement.

For [[Amadea]] it seemed almost regal, finer even than the already well-appointed stalls around it. She didn't know the history as well as [[Artus]] did, but from her memory of his stories, this one had to be one of the valuable carts moving from the lands of the [[Great Expanse]], somewhere in the far, distant west.

Along its side panel ran a symbol [[Amadea]] too half-recognized — it was the same symbol that the adults in the caravan clutched every time the nights got darkest, the sigil they pressed into wooden pendants or scratched into tent posts.

It was a clear line that began below and ended above — a line that somewhere in its upper half had found a circle, as though the line had pierced straight through it on its way toward the sky, and carried the shape of it still.

She didn't know what it meant. Only that it meant something important to people who still needed to believe things did — in the same way the [[Ballad]]s meant something to [[Artus]], and the way those same [[Ballad]]s, through him, had come to mean something to her.

The merchant himself was elsewhere. The cart was not unguarded, but its guard had been drawn three stalls down by a commotion that had nothing to do with [[Artus]], which meant it had everything to do with him.

But even through the spectacle, the guards caught him with both hands inside a traveling strongbox bolted beneath the cart's bench.

What happened next was not like the other times. This was not a tired merchant's corrective fury. These were men who guarded things of real value, it was evident from the intricate details of gold and white on the very rim of the cart.

[[Amadea]] had a glimpse at what [[Artus]] had grabbed just slightly before the tall hooded men forced him to the ground. It was not bread, not dried meat, not even one of those fabled golden [[Auric Peach]]es. No, it was not anything that lived in the language of survival. 

She saw it was a fragment of something — glass, or what looked like a sheet of glass, no larger than the leather pouch he kept the seed in, set inside a lined wooden case beside many instruments, golden bells, and sealed containers that had no business in any frontier market she had ever seen.

Then the guards closed in around him, and [[Artus]] was gone from her sight.

She would only hear the full account of it later — second-hand, from the halting way [[Artus]] described it, still turning the memory over as though trying to understand what he had touched. He said it caught the torchlight wrong. Not reflecting it, instead almost like fracturing it inward, pulling it apart into something quieter, colder, and more complex than light had any right to be inside a thin sheet.

He said it was as if the surface was not a surface at all, but depth. As if looking into it were less like looking at a thing, and more like being seen by one.

He hadn't meant to pick it up. He'd reached for the case beside it. But his hand had found the fragment first, and for a moment — just a moment — he said the cold had stopped feeling like cold when his hand touched it. It had become instead something choral, layered, and still, as though a hundred voices had always been folded into the surface as quiet lights muffled just beneath his fingertips.

The guards were not interested in his explanation.

They kept him for more than two hours.

[[Amadea]] waited in the dry bed of a stream east of the market quarter, exactly as they had arranged. But she was counting each second. She counted time the way children in crisis count time — not by thought, but by the body's inventory of its own dread.

The light changed. The sky moved through its slow dusk, going the color it always went: old bruises, cooling embers.

He came back eventually.

She heard him before she saw him — not by sound, but by the quality of silence around a person carrying a debilitating pain quietly. He came down the embankment one careful step at a time, with a ragged, chaotic breath, moving with the economy of someone who had located exactly which positions still functioned.

She rose.

He lifted a hand, and she waited until he came to rest against the streambed wall with a breath he controlled through visible effort.

His face was wrong.

Not just because of the split lip and bruised ribs of the other times. There was a cut above his left eye still seeping at the edges, his jaw was swollen along one side where something harder than a hand had landed more than once.

The way he held his left arm flat against his side told her what it always told her — except this time the story was worse than it had ever been.

The way his breathing moved in his chest confirmed it. This time, it was a permanent wound, and she could feel herself learning that distinction with the cold precision that had taught her everything else she knew about loss.

She said nothing. There were no words adequate to what she was feeling — not grief, not anger, perhaps something colder: the world was capable of this. She had been in that town with him, with the caravan, no one was there to save him, and neither of those facts could be undone.

[[Artus]] reached into his coat that still had stains of dry blood.

He drew out a copper circlet — small, decorative, the kind sold to village girls for feast-day celebrations in the [[Ballad]]s. It had twisted wire, small settings for colored glass, although most of it was gone, the frame bent slightly out of round. Not worth anything to anyone with something better. She thought he must have snatched it from a market stall on his way out. It was cheap, and damaged, and entirely unmistakable as a gift.

He held it over her head with one careful hand, the other still pressed to his side, and [[Amadea]] was already crying.

Not loudly. She had learned long before to make her grief silent. Tears without sound, in the same way water finds the lowest channel. She did not look away from the cut above his brow.

"You're hurt," 

She finally broke the silence.

"Stop," He said. Not unkindly.

"Again," She said, in a trembling voice. "Because of me." 

"No, because I chose to be." He kept the circlet above her hair. "There is a difference."

"It doesn't feel different."

"It does from where I'm standing." His voice was even — not the voice of [[Ballad]]s, but the raw voice of a confession he had been carrying for long. 

"You keep confusing cost with failure. Something costing something doesn't mean it went wrong. It just means what it was worth in its price."

She didn't answer. The tears kept going.

He lowered the circlet onto her head. Too large. It slid slightly to one side, and he straightened it with two careful fingers, as if he were righting a painting in a house that he decided to make beautiful.

"You're a princess," He said.

She almost laughed through the tears. "I'm not."

"You are. You just don't know it yet." The same even voice. "And one day, you will be an empress."

"You can't see the future."

"No, but I can see a future. It might be wrong. But mine is better than no future at all." 

He settled back against the streambed wall, looking at the distant sky, which was doing its usual dusk trick of turning the color of old bruises.

"And empresses don't sit in dry riverbeds crying over stolen circlets. It's not dignified."

"I'm not crying over the circlet."

"I know." He paused. "You're crying because someone got hurt, and you think it's your fault. But it isn't. It's the world's fault. The world is broken, not you." 

He looked at her sideways. "Save the crying for things that deserve it. The small stuff — let it go. Empresses have to be strategic about their grief."

She looked at him for a long moment. He was sitting in a dry streambed with a cracked rib and a bleeding brow, with absolute [[Composure]] and not one ounce of intention of making that into a tragedy. As if the blood were weather. As if the circlet on her tilted head were simply the coronation for the correct arrangement.

She stopped crying.

She took the circlet off and held it in both hands. Damaged, bent, most of its glass gone. It had cost her nothing except his pain, which was everything.

"I'm going to keep this," She said.

"I thought you would."

She looked up. "But when I build the city. When the story ends the way you keep saying it ends." She held the circlet up, high between them. "I'm going to be the one who puts this on your head."

He looked at the circlet. Then at her. Something moved across his face — a sort of startled recognition, as though she had named something he had felt but not yet dared to say aloud, even when performing the [[Ballad]]s.

"Alright," He said.

"I mean it."

"I know." He rose carefully, one hand finding the streambed wall, and extended the other to pull her up.

"I really know you mean it."

#### Act 4. [[The Golden Light in the Sky]] and the [[Auroral Ribbons]] 
#### Act 5. The Dawn That Drowned In Fire

**Draft Piece On Fires**

The caravan went wrong slowly.

Not at in a flash at once but over the next three [[Moon]]s, with the one thing neither [[Artus]] or her could've expected.

[[Amadea]] had learned every kind of wrong that arrived fast, whether the sweetness before the static, the tremors before the dread, or the breathless quiet of dusk when something at the edges was already full and feeding. Those she had learned through survival before anything could even beg a question, but they had a pattern: wrong had a shape, and the shape announced itself.

She had organized her entire understanding of threat around that legibility.

Around the decency of warning.

But of course, this had neither. Which was, in its own way, the most frightening thing about despair. It is brutally effective at deceiving, and despondency, too, spreads like a plague.

It all began with the caravan gathering fires, or rather, the lack of them.

Whenever the caravan stopped to rest, the gathering fires always followed. [[Amadea]] thought of them as background static, but they were unique in the sense that each of them had its orbit, its people, and a set of unspoken rules about what terms were even approached to discuss new locations. Nothing of that had changed. What changed was the volume between those gatherings. The last three days were only silence. It seemed as if laughter, the shared complaints or the voice of any kind, had itself drowned.

It was oddly, unnervingly silent at first.

Then, slowly, the silence began to change its shape.

Not into speech. Not at first. Into something that lived just beneath speech — in the angle of a shoulder turned away where it had always been open, in a hand that closed over a portion a half-second before releasing it, in the particular quality of two people walking near each other without acknowledging the nearness. Small enough to dismiss. She didn't dismiss it.

She noticed it on Artus too — on the way he moved through his plays with the adults. There was a wrongness in the register, a note in the wrong key that had once landed clean and now became a recurring exchange of insults before the adults could reach her in the crossfire. Twenty-three marks. The inn will take eight. The road east will take three in tolls if the checkpoint guards are honest and six if they're not. There is another [[Indulgent Enclave]] three days east with an actual stage and a contracted audience and a steward who has already sent word that the Violet Actress is expected.

They will chant the name again. They will not know what it protects.

The seed is in my coat pocket. The city is still east of something I haven't found yet but should be close to the reach of my real destination.

I pick up the case and walk to sleep.

I need the energy. The next crossing will be through an [[Age of Embers]] waste.
