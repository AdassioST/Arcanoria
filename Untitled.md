He cleared his throat, glanced once at the front of the wagon where the coughing had finally settled into a ragged, shallow rhythm, and began to sing — a ballad about a Mythical Virtuoso of Resonance, of a hand that could pull music from silence itself and mend what silence had broken. She huffed something that might have been a laugh, if either of them had the strength left for a real one.

Inside, Corvin sat with a strip of parchment and a stub of charcoal, doing the quiet arithmetic of survival — what remained, what was needed, what could be stretched thin enough to hold until the next town, the next Enclave, the next miracle. Amadea watched him from across the flame until her eyes grew too heavy to keep open, and the last thing she saw before sleep took her was the ring on his hand, still faintly glowing, even in stillness.

Later, she woke to the shifting warmth of the fire outside, curled against the woman's side near the buffalo, the animals' slow breathing a kind of lullaby neither of them needed to ask for.

## Event 2 — Dawn of Day 2

The coughing woke her before the light did.

It came from close by, shallow and wet, each breath sounding like it had to be excavated rather than drawn. The woman beside her — Elaine, Amadea would learn her name properly soon enough — slept on, exhausted past the reach of the sound. But Amadea's eyes opened, and across the dying coals of the fire, she saw Corvin sitting with the coughing boy, his voice pitched too low for her to catch the words.

She watched the shape of the boy grow smaller against Corvin's arm. Not smaller in size — smaller in the way a candle grows smaller as it burns, some essential portion of it consumed and gone. Corvin murmured something to the little brass pendulum in his palm, a sound that might have been a prayer dressed as a habit, and then he wrapped his arms fully around the boy and held him the way you hold something you already know you are about to lose.

He carried him into the dark beyond the firelight.

He did not come back with him.

Amadea lay still, watching the space where they had disappeared, feeling something in her chest tighten around the shape of an absence she did not yet have the vocabulary to name grief for. When Corvin returned, alone, his silhouette moved slower than before, as though the walk back weighed more than the walk out.

He knelt by the shirt he'd left folded near the fire, drew a knife from his boot, and cut a small, careful square from the fabric. He worked with the precision of a man performing a ritual rather than a chore, tucking the linen into the lining of his coat with fingers that trembled only slightly.

Then he lifted his ringed hand, and where there had been nothing a moment before, a cello now rested against his shoulder — summoned whole from the glowing band on his finger, its wood the deep amber of something oak-worn and well-loved. He drew the bow across it, and a low, mourning note rolled out into the grey dawn.

Amadea crept closer, drawn by the sound the way she might once have been drawn to warmth.

"Why the cloth?" she asked, when the note faded.

Corvin didn't startle this time. He'd have felt her approach; a man like him always felt approach.

"This linen," he said, not looking up from the strings, "is because someone should remember."

She didn't understand it fully. But she understood enough — that a life had ended, and that ending had been given a shape too small to see but real enough to keep.

She left him to it and went to wake Artus, who slept curled in the second cart below, one arm slung over his lute like it might wander off without him. She was halfway down the wooden steps when she heard it — a low, wrong sound threading through the birdless quiet. A growl built from hunger rather than anger, thin and rattling, like breath dragged over broken glass.

She turned.

It came out of the treeline slow and stiff-legged, a wolf-shaped thing with its ribs showing through matted, patchy fur, its eyes fixed on her with the flat, singular focus of something that had not eaten in longer than it could remember and had decided, with the last of its reasoning, that she would do.

Her pulse climbed before her mind caught up to why. And underneath it, faint at first, then rising, that old sound began to bleed back in — the static, the buzzing nothing that had swallowed her whole after the river, after her mother's hand went still in hers. It filled her ears just as the creature's muscles coiled to lunge.

She couldn't move. She couldn't hear anything but the roar of her own unraveling mind.

Then something crashed down from above her — a shape falling from the upper deck of the wagon like a stone loosed from a sling, and Corvin's shoulder met the creature's ribs mid-leap with a sound like a door slamming shut on a storm. The two of them went down together in a tangle of claw and coat, and in the same motion, Corvin drove the lower spike of his cello — the endpin, sharpened like something built as much for killing as for music — straight through the beast's chest.

It didn't bleed. It dissolved, unraveling into grey motes that scattered on the wind like ash from a fire no one had lit.

And with it, the static in Amadea's skull went silent.

The world rushed back into her all at once, and it did not rush in as static this time — it rushed in as _feeling_. She felt the grass first, soft yet firm beneath the sole of her shoe, and the bark of a nearby tree splintering gently under her fingertips as she reached out without meaning to. The path ahead ran on for what looked like miles, deep green grass lining its edges, and she walked a few unsteady steps along it, letting the tender, cool wind pass over her skin like a hand smoothing down something that had been standing on end. Her muscles weren't tensed anymore — she hadn't known, until that moment, that they had been. Above her, gaps in the branches let pale, gentle moonlight fall through in patches, and she breathed a heavy sigh that bled through her chest like spilled ink. She was finally free of the dissonance.

Corvin rose from the dissolving ash, brushing dirt from his knees, and bent to pick something small and glinting from where the creature had been. A seed, crystalline, catching the early light like a drop of frozen dew — a Rose Seed.

"The worst part about having to bid farewell," he said, turning it over once in his fingers before folding it into her palm, "is not even the death itself. It's making sure we aren't hunted shortly after."

He collapsed the cello back into the ring with a flick of his wrist, and looked at her with something that wasn't quite gentleness but wasn't unkind either.

"The world always takes," he said. "The answer is to take back."

She carried the seed back down to Artus like a soldier returning from a campaign, and when she held it out to him, his eyes went wide and delighted.

"You're definitely the undefeatable violet empress," he declared, "if you got one of these yourself." He turned it in the light, already composing something behind his eyes. "I've got an idea for today's Ballad because of this."

The idea didn't survive contact with memory. His face fell a moment later as the fuller shape of the morning caught up to him — the boy from last night's song, the boy he had been trying to cheer, was not in the wagon anymore and never would be again.

They spent the rest of that day picking through the ruins nearby anyway, gathering branches, scraps of wire, anything the wasteland hadn't already claimed, Artus quietly determined that whatever he played tonight would be good enough that no one else would be taken by the coughing again — as though a ballad, played well enough, could hold a line against a world that did not negotiate.

## Event 3 — Dusk to Night of Day 2

By dusk, Artus had set up his lute again, the Rose Seed balanced carefully on a flat stone in front of him like a prop borrowed from a much grander stage. He'd barely drawn the first chord when the ground shuddered beneath them.

It came from far off at first — a cascade of light igniting the horizon in violent, flickering bursts, static criticality tearing through the distance like lightning without a storm to justify it. Then came the tremor, low and rolling, and beneath it, the howling. Not one voice. Dozens, then more, layering into a single ragged chorus of hunger closing in from the wastes.

The adults were moving before the sound had finished registering.

"Kay!" Corvin barked, and the man who'd teased him the night before appeared at Artus's side in an instant, crouching low.

"Give it here," Kay said, already reaching for the seed on its stone.

"But it's—"

"I know. I know what it is." His voice gentled even as his hands moved with brutal efficiency, packing gear, kicking dirt over the fire. "The horror's got to be dealt with physically before it can be dealt with emotionally, kid. But you should always mourn the fallen — just not right this second."

He drove his palm into the earth, and a spear of pale crystal erupted from the ground beneath it, tall and glassy. With quick, practiced motion he carved a shallow furrow at its base, pressed the Rose Seed into the soil, and covered it over.

"These are meant to be planted always," he said, already turning to gather the rest of the camp, "preferably wherever you found them."

They tore the camp down with the efficiency of people who had done it a hundred times under worse conditions. Elaine and Corvin argued in short, clipped bursts over the map — whether to risk the doomed settlement nearby for any children who might still be inside it, or to abandon the idea entirely and run.

"We just lost one," Corvin said, voice flat with the particular exhaustion of a man who had made this argument before and hated that he had to make it again. "We are not gambling the ones we still have for the ones we might not save."

"Corvin—"

"Elaine, listen to those hounds. That's not a handful. That's a whole starved pack, and it's between us and that settlement either way."

Kay's voice cut through before Elaine could answer, backing Corvin without hesitation, though his jaw was tight with the cost of it. "He's right. We plow straight through what's coming, or we don't get anywhere at all." He and Corvin argued a moment longer over which route out — the ridge trail or the valley floor — before settling, sharply, on the valley, where a mountain pass opened onto an Outpost on the far side.

They scrambled into formation. Elaine took the center cart, snapping the lead line taut in her grip, the buffalo already stamping and snorting at the smell of what was coming. Corvin swung up beside Amadea on the left cart. Kay pulled Artus up onto the right beside him.

Amadea climbed the narrow steps to the upper deck as Corvin readied his instrument, and from the rear, watching the tree line fill with shapes, she asked him plainly what they were fighting.

"Remember the one from before?" he said, not looking away from the dark. "That's an Atonalis. Cloths of hunger — the undigested pain of the world that grew teeth, claws, and learned how to feed. These ones in particular are Carnalix. It's up to us to put them down before they take us instead."

Elaine rose at the front of her cart, lifted her violin, and loosed a single blinding flare of light from the strings out across the valley, illuminating what waited for them in one merciless flash.

"Yeah," she called back over her shoulder, voice grim. "No chance of going in silent. There's a whole horde. Nascent, all of them, but there's a _lot_ of nascent."

She struck a pose from her ring, the violin catching a strange glow along its neck, and cracked the lead line hard. "Buckle up!"

The buffalo lunged forward as one, and the caravan dropped down the ladder-steep descent into the valley below.

"Corvin — give me some Resonance!"

"What? Why? We're not setting up a field—"

"Give her a beat, you idiot!" Kay shouted from the other cart, already swinging his polearm loose. "Start playing something!"

Corvin's expression shifted instantly, the confusion burning off into focus. He wedged the little brass pendulum into the crook of his elbow, letting it swing free as a makeshift metronome. "Aight — third of Cindergale." He set the ticking mechanism with a flick of his thumb. "On one-eighteen."

The device locked into rhythm, and Corvin drew his bow across the cello in one long, driving stroke, unleashing a heavy, rolling bassline of fire that rolled out over the valley floor ahead of them like a second sunrise.

The music didn't stay behind them. It became the shape of the chase itself.

The caravan plunged into the pack, carving a path through snapping jaws and starved bodies, the warm rush of Corvin's flame washing over Amadea's face in time with the beat. As they split to dodge a jagged outcrop, a massive Sectile lunged, jaws wide enough to take Corvin's whole shoulder — and Elaine's violin shrieked a single, blinding note of pure light directly into its eyes, close enough that Amadea felt the heat of the near-miss on her own skin.

"You owe my light one!" Elaine called out, weaving her cart hard to the left without missing a note. "I expect it back repaid by midnight!"

The valley floor became something between a battlefield and a stage — three carts weaving in a wide triangle, swapping positions with a precision that looked less like survival and more like choreography. Kay fought at the perimeter with brutal, economical violence, his crystallized polearm spinning and striking in time with Corvin's bassline, each impact landing like a drumbeat woven into the piece. Elaine's light bent and refracted around his strikes, giving every thrust an edge sharp enough to cut through bone and starlight both.

Through the gap between the carts, Amadea caught sight of Artus pressed to the upper window of the far wagon, wide-eyed, utterly transfixed by the sight of Kay's polearm dancing through the dark — the slashes falling into perfect time with the violin and cello, a language of violence and rhythm neither of the children fully understood but both of them felt in their chests like a second pulse.

The wheels drummed against the earth. The buffalo's hooves thundered. Elaine's whip cracked between notes. Kay's blade rang against claw and bone in a rhythm that never once broke. Amadea watched them make the world sing, and watched the world answer back in fire and light and steel.

As they closed on the narrow crevice that marked the valley's exit, the formation tightened — single file now, Elaine at the front, Kay holding the middle, Corvin anchoring the rear.

The horde thickened faster than they could outrun it. Bodies began clawing at the edges of the rear cart, close enough now that Corvin's flame alone wasn't holding the line.

He paused mid-phrase, drew a small blade from his belt, and cut a clean line across his own palm.

Blood welled instantly, and he pressed it flat against the strings before drawing the bow again — this time with a furious, desperate momentum that tore his fingertips open within moments, blood mixing visibly with the vibration of the strings. The orange of his flame deepened, curdled, and rose into a devastating, saturated crimson, hotter and hungrier than anything he'd summoned before.

At the same moment, Kay abandoned his stance at the perimeter entirely, using a burst of Resonance to leap from the rear cart to the lead in a single bound. He caught Elaine mid-note, spinning her out of her driving stance and into his arms, and what followed was not quite a rescue and not quite a dance — both of them moving together in the small space atop the middle cart, their magic beginning to intertwine as naturally as breath.

It ended, as these things sometimes do without warning, in a kiss — desperate, unplanned, and entirely too honest for the middle of a massacre.

Their combined emotion detonated outward into a burst of prismatic light that staggered the nearest wave of the horde, and from it, Kay's crystal magic bent into a sharp, gleaming V, fusing with Elaine's refracted light along the flanks of the formation. The three carts, impossibly, began to move as one shape — a single massive lance, Corvin's crimson fire roaring at the rear, Kay's crystal forming its lethal point, Elaine's light wrapping the sides in a blinding sheath.

"Lovebirds!" Corvin bellowed over the roar, condensing his flame into a single, tightening ball of heat balanced at the tip of his bow. "The horde is still closing! I need you _here_. Give me a sling — aim for the towering dead tree up to your left. Bar sixteen, no idea how many pulses, but you'll see the blue glow beneath it!"

Elaine pulled back from Kay, straightening her hat with a hurried hand. "Right! Sorry!"

Kay blinked, clearly having caught none of it. "Where? Didn't catch that — I was busy."

"_Bar sixteen. Left. Glowing dead tree,_" Corvin snarled.

Kay grinned despite himself and slammed his palm into the ground, conjuring a long ramp of crystal angled up and to the left. "Left you some holes on the sides, big man."

Corvin funneled the fireball down the length of the wind-carved corridor, over the ramp, and the massive orb detonated against the base of the ancient dead tree in a whirling column of fire. The tree groaned, split, and toppled — a wall of burning wreckage crashing down directly across the mouth of the crevice behind them, sealing it in an instant beneath a curtain of flame and falling timber.

The screeching of the horde cut off abruptly, muffled behind the wreckage, and the battered caravan burst free of the valley into the open, quiet dark on the other side.

## Aftermath — The Outpost Ruins

They found shelter in the bones of an old Outpost a few hours later, its walls half-collapsed but still enough to break the wind. No one spoke much while they took stock of the damage — Kay running his hands lightly along Elaine's arms, checking for wounds she waved off before he'd finished asking, Corvin sitting apart from the others with a small vial of pale green leaves pressed against the raw, torn skin of his own hands.

Near the stream that cut through the ruin's edge, Elaine knelt behind Amadea, working wet fingers through her tangled hair, sectioning it slowly into two even braids. Amadea sat still, watching the water, feeling the ache of the day finally settle into her small shoulders now that there was nothing left to run from.

"You're scared of this place," Elaine said, not a question.

Amadea didn't answer, which was its own answer.

Elaine's fingers moved to the end of the left braid, working through a knot far more deliberate and complicated than the simple ones she'd tied on the right — a pattern of loops that took real care, real memory, to recreate.

"If you ever lose your way in the dark," Elaine said quietly, tying it off, "trust the person who knows exactly how to tie this knot."

Amadea reached back and touched it once, memorizing the shape of it with her fingertips the way she'd memorized the sound of a cello and the weight of a ring.

Elaine straightened, wiped her hands on her skirts, and turned toward Corvin with the particular look of a woman calling in a debt.

"Remember that favor," she said, "from earlier? Against the Sectile? I'm calling it in." She nodded at the astrolabe tucked in his coat. "Come here and use that thing for something useful."

Corvin sighed, long and put-upon, but he came anyway, kneeling beside Amadea and lifting the little pendulum toward the sky, angling it this way and that, murmuring under his breath as he tried to read some pattern in the stars that refused to resolve itself. He tried twice more, failed twice more, and finally lowered it with a shake of his head.

"I don't really remember how to do this part properly," he admitted. Then, after a moment: "Doesn't matter, though. She's not on her first cycle — she's not four. And she's not on her third — she's not twelve." He looked at Amadea, and something in his tired face softened. "How about we just start celebrating now that she's awake. We'll figure out the rest in the morning, once I've had some sleep." He glanced pointedly at Elaine. "Just be merciful. I did have to wake up early."

## Event 4 — The Outpost Settlement, A Few Days Later

A few days passed in the slow, careful rhythm of recovery — wounds closing, wheels repaired, the caravan inching forward again through a wasteland grown momentarily quiet.

Corvin and Kay sparred in the open ground beside the carts one afternoon, wooden practice blades cracking against each other in quick, controlled exchanges, and Artus watched every motion with open, hungry fascination, mimicking footwork half a beat behind whenever he thought no one was looking.

When the two men finally broke apart, breathing hard and grinning at each other like boys rather than men who'd burned half a horde alive days earlier, Corvin turned to the children with a different kind of mission in mind.

"There's a settlement half a day from here," he said. "I need you two to go find Auric Peaches. The gold ones, if you can — the name doesn't lie about which are best." He crouched to Artus's height. "Think of it as gathering props. For that Ballad you never finished."

The Outpost, when they reached it, was larger than Amadea expected — walled, guarded, wary of strangers in the particular way places got when they had survived by being wary. Artus, true to form, could not lie to save his life; his face gave away every half-truth before it left his mouth. Amadea could.

She spun the story easily, borrowing the name of a settlement she'd half-caught from Elaine and Corvin's argument in the valley — the doomed one they'd chosen not to risk everything for. Refugees, she told the guard at the gate, gathering supplies for the Ballads they performed to keep spirits up. Artus, hearing the lie built on top of something almost true, leaned into the part that _was_ true — the Ballads, the music, the real hope behind the fabrication — and the guard's suspicion visibly eased at the sincerity in his voice, even as it lingered on hers.

The guard's hand shot out and closed around her wrist, hard enough that she felt the bruise forming before he'd even finished asking his question again, testing her for the flinch that would give her away.

She didn't look away. She didn't break the story, didn't let her voice waver, didn't let anything in her small, steady face admit to a single lie. Absolute certainty, worn like armor she hadn't known she owned until she needed it.

The guard released her wrist and waved them through, muttering something about wasted time, and they left with an entire crate of pink Auric Peaches balanced between them — and, hidden beneath the topmost layer where neither the guard nor the children had noticed until they were well clear of the gate, a single true golden peach, glowing faintly even in the shade.

Artus noticed the bruise on her wrist before she could hide it fully. He said nothing about it, only quietly took the heavier end of the crate for the rest of the walk back, carrying two-handed what should have taken one.

When they returned, Corvin lifted the golden peach from the crate with something close to reverence, turning it in the light.

"Thanks to the heavens," he murmured, "we get a real one. It's been so long since I've seen one of these."

He decided, without needing to be asked, that it belonged to Amadea — a birthday gift, and a crown for the girl clever and steady enough to walk out of an Outpost with an entire crate secured on nothing but nerve and a well-told lie.

That evening, Corvin sliced the pink peaches into halves, hollowing them with careful, practiced motions into makeshift bowls — a trick from somewhere and someone he didn't name, though something in his voice when he described the method suggested it belonged to a memory he still carried carefully, the way he carried the linen sewn into his coat. He filled them with whatever the caravan had managed to scrape together, and they were, against every reasonable expectation, delicious.

Amadea ate from the single golden peach set aside just for her, the juice running warm over her fingers, while Artus finished, at last, the Ballad of the Mythical Virtuoso he'd been carrying since the night the coughing boy had died — the melody resolving, this time, into something that sounded almost like hope.

She sat in the firelight, sticky-fingered and bruised-wristed and, for the first time since the river had taken her mother's hand from hers, something close to warm.

The universe, she was beginning to understand, was catastrophically broken. It took without asking, and it kept taking, and no amount of wanting otherwise would change the shape of that truth.

But if you were willing to lie convincingly enough, and fight hard enough, and stand your ground when a stranger's hand closed hard around your wrist — you could still carve out a moment like this one, small and golden and entirely your own, right in the middle of all that ruin.
