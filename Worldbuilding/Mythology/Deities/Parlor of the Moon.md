#story

Also known as "The Celestial Paradise" as revealed later in the Sonata of [[Arcanoria]]. is an elaborate idyllic palace suspended in the firmament between [[Arcanoria]] and [[The Infinite Void]] originally meant to be the dual realm of the [[Auric Aria]] and [[Selenea]].

It is a complex construct built from [[Auric Geometry]] that has many seven main chambers within related to one of the threads of the [[Auric Heptacode]]. They central to the Sonata of [[Arcanoria]] and are progressively revealed on the movements of the Sonata. In this palace, the wings of [[Mastery Over Chaos]] belong to the [[Auric Aria]], [[Potential of Creation]] belong to [[Selenea]], and the [[Key of Attunement]], the Garden of Life or Eden, belongs to both as a promise of their collaboration.

- The Crescent Court ([[Flux]]): Located on the center, it is the entrance and hall of the [[Parlor of the Moon]], and effectively "[[Selenea]]'s Prison of the [[Moon]]." This is the first chamber of the [[Parlor of the Moon]], it contains the outer gardens, [[Selenea]]'s concert hall, the greenhouse atelier, and the crescent archway entrance. This is the place [[Selenea]] has been trapped in because she couldn't solve the puzzle of [[Auric Geometry]] of the lower or upper set of stairs.

- The Golden Observatory ([[Luminance]]): Located on the west wing of the upper floors.

- The Infinite Chorus Halls ([[Void]]): Located on the east wing of the upper floors.

- The Myriad Gallery ([[Strand]]): Located on the west wing of the lower floors.

- The Mirrored Waterfall ([[Crystal]]): Located on the east wing of the lower floors.

- The Heart Core ([[Cindergale]]): Located on the very bottom of the [[Parlor of the Moon]]. This is the core of the [[Resonance Box]] that contains the inscription of how love and collaboration was meant to be the fire that sustains [[Arcanoria]].

- The Garden of Life ([[Resonance]]): Located on the very apex of the palace, it is the true Paradise that the [[Auric Aria]] wanted to build originally with [[Selenea]], it contains on the very center the tree of all life, the very first [[Fated Flower]] ever created that grew into a massive fruit tree filled with the purest version of [[Auric Peach]]es, and [[Silver Blooms]].

This massive celestial folding palace was the original vision of the Paradise the [[Auric Aria]] was building, of a world built on [[Consonance]]. The [[Parlor of the Moon]] was originally supposed to be the celestial throne that the [[Auric Aria]] made for her and [[Selenea]] to overview [[Arcanoria]]. Tragically, the eons of guilt and shame made the [[Auric Aria]] repress the memory entirely, and she has dissociative amnesia on the very fabric of the place she built for [[Selenea]] out of love.

Moreover, it gets explained in the Sonata that the [[Parlor of the Moon]] was in fact a test, and [[Selenea]] wasn't wrong, but the [[Auric Aria]] weaponized both her jealousy and the lack of understanding of [[Selenea]], There was an exit if she was able to navigate until the Garden of Life. "Aria explained Auric Geometry to Selenea enthusiastically throughout the Sixth Cycle, but Selenea couldn't fully comprehend the entirety of it." And Aria was hurt, but it's partially self inflicted because Aria's enthusiasm was not teaching. It was performance—a display of her own mastery that left Selenea feeling inadequate rather than educated. Aria wanted a student who would reflect her brilliance back to her, not a student who would struggle and ask questions and reveal the gaps in her pedagogy.

When Selenea failed to learn, Aria did not change her approach. She withdrew. She built a puzzle and called it a prison. She told herself she was giving Selenea a chance, but she was really abandoning her to her own inadequacy—the same abandonment pattern that defines Aria's entire relationship with creation. Selenea was originally right, it was in fact a test, but she was wrong to fixate on escape. The Parlor was not a cage; it was a curriculum. The stairs were not exits; they were lessons that the [[Auric Aria]] wanted her forcefully to learn. Her obsession with leaving prevented her from learning the geometry that would have allowed her to leave.

Moreover [[Selenea]] believes she was imprisoned because she wasn't smart enough. But the truth is more painful: she was imprisoned because she stopped trying to become smart enough. She retreated into fantasy, into daydreams, into the comfort of imagining a better world rather than the labor of understanding this one.

This is not victim-blaming. [[Selenea]] was wronged. But her response to being wronged—to retreat into helplessness and self-blame—is itself a form of wrongness. Not moral wrongness, but strategic wrongness. The stairs existed. She could have found them. She chose not to look. She had the capacity. What she lacked was confidence and the willingness to abandon her fantasy of escape in favor of the slow, painful work of learning.

This is seen as well in her [[Motif Awakening]] Wish: "I wish I could create again, with her, with my children." Not "I wish I could understand finally the [[Auric Geometry]] the [[Auric Aria]] wants to teach me." She wanted the result of the understanding—collaboration, creation, connection—without the painful process of acquiring the understanding itself that the [[Auric Aria]] desperately craved from [[Selenea]] to see her as a true peer and not merely a shadow that would follow her aimlessly whenever she led somewhere. And the worst part is that [[Selenea]] premise on the test was right, but she gave up halfway waiting for the [[Auric Aria]] to rescue her.

### The Inscriptions of Purpose
_"I hope that you can find the exit to the garden I built for us."_

The mirrored palace was never to be used a prison, the [[Parlor of the Moon]] was designed to be the test that sustained [[Arcanoria]], and these inscriptions are the [[Auric Aria]]'s own words that have been forgotten to guilt and time, they are progressively revealed within the movements of Memory, Rage, and Forgiveness, with the very final inscription in the Garden of Paradise before the [[Law of Relics]] can be used in the fabric of the [[Known Universe]].

**First Reveal. At the beginning of Memory when the Crescent Court unfolds**  
The [[Celestial Ensnaring Mirror]] does not extract vitality—it is a massive [[Resonance]] engine supposed to be fueled with emotional authenticity. The [[Auric Aria]] chose [[Selenea]] not to punish her, but because [[Selenea]] was the most emotionally authentic being she knew, even more than the [[Auric Aria]] herself. The tragedy was not merely malice that built due to envy, but rather silence that came from the [[Auric Aria]]'s [[Impostor Syndrome]] festering at the lowest, weaponizing her feelings for her, and never explained, and [[Selenea]] assumed the worst while giving up.

*The walls pulse with deep blue, wet with emotion. The text is written in the [[Auric Aria]]'s earliest hand—more hopeful, less burdened.*

> *"The [[Parlor of the Moon]] is a massive [[Resonance Box]] built on feeling.*
>
> *The first chamber, attuned to Flux, is the key. Emotion is not the byproduct of the palace. It is the fuel. And it must be real.*
>
> *I have chosen [[Selenea]] for this role. She is the most emotionally authentic being I know. She feels truly. She will not need to perform. Her natural state will sustain the world. And I believe she will have the easiest lesson by feeling instead of learning [[Mastery Over Chaos]]. I hope you can learn to read by connection.*
>
> *I do not tell her this because I am afraid she will say no. I am afraid she will not want to be here. But she is the only one who can do this. I can't leave anything else to chance or [[Arcanoria]] will never be complete.*
>
> *I am sorry. I will be sorry forever. But the world needs her feelings, and her feelings are real, and that is enough. I hope you can realize what I meant for you."*

**Second Reveal (Heart Core)**  
The Mirror was never meant for Selenea alone. It was a **shared throne**—a resonant chamber designed for both goddesses to take turns anchoring the world. The Aria built it as a classroom where they would learn to speak each other's language. But after the Aria emerged from her first turn. Her cowardice curdled into betrayal: she left Selenea inside and fled, telling herself the world needed Selenea's feelings more than her own.

> _"This is the heart of our throne._
>
>_I built the paradise as a place where one of us may rest while the other weaves. The construct and all life below are maintained by the resonance of whichever one of us sits within. The Mirror amplifies whatever emotion the occupant genuinely feels—grief, joy, longing, devotion—and transmutes it into the Dual Confluence Stream that sustains Arcanoria._
>
_Selenea and I are meant to take turns. One will weave above while the other anchors below. We will share the burden. We will never be alone._
>
_I will go first. To show her it does not hurt. To prove it is not a punishment. Then she will take her turn, and I will watch from above, and we will learn to trust each other's absence as much as each other's presence._
>
_This is the covenant. This is the monument of how we become partners, not just collaborators._
>
_I name this place the Heart Core because it holds the pulse of everything we are building together."_

**Third Reveal (Myriad Gallery Records)**  
The Mirror runs on whatever emotion the occupant genuinely feels—grief, longing, joy, devotion. Selenea's suffering powered Arcanoria not because suffering was required, but because she *believed* she was imprisoned. The Aria cannot free her now without collapsing the Dual Confluence Stream and ending all life. Nor can she simply hand Selenea the answer to the geometry, because that would invalidate the lesson and return them to square zero—the Aria knowing, Selenea trusting without understanding.

## The Final Subversion

The Sonata does not offer a solution. It offers **imperfect trying**. The Aria cannot fix the past. She can only show up—visit the Parlor, bring Civilization as witness, sit with Selenea in the impossibility. The lesson she wanted to teach—that imperfect trying is better than perfect silence—is finally learned by both of them, at the cost of eons of suffering.

### **Part I. The Golden Tether**

She has not been waiting with dignity. She has been waiting—the way a child waits for a parent who promised they would return, the way a lover waits for a letter that never comes, the way someone left behind learns to make a game of absence because the alternative is madness.

The parlor is not a room. It is a moon-palace suspended in the firmament, between [[Arcanoria]], and [[The Infinite Void]]. A crescent of stone and starlight hanging over the world. Slender pillars ring an open court, their tops lost in the dark, and from the heart of the crescent a fountain spills Lunehymn in silver sheets. The water runs in narrow channels along the arc, then thins into a falling veil that feeds the hanging gardens below.

Selenea sits by the fountain, bare feet in the cold Lunehymn.

Her hair falls in long, dark-blue waves that catch specks of light like captured stars, threaded with thin gold strands that twist into tiny constellations at her temples. A sheer, moon-wet gown clings to her, off one shoulder, fabric shimmering with the same pale glow as the Lunehymn. At her throat, a black lace choker clings like a tattoo of shadow, and in its center burns her [[Soul Leitmotif]]—a small ovoid sigil shaped from overlapping circles and lines. It pulses faintly, keeping time with a heart she keeps clinging to not forget she has.

She has turned the palace into a nest. Half-finished sculptures gather in the shadowed recesses between pillars. Silver threads lie in tangled constellations across the stone. Pools of Lunehymn have collected in shallow basins, reflecting the same memories again and again because she cannot stop replaying them.

Below the crescent, the hanging gardens step down in terraces carved from pale stone. Glimmerfern spills over every ledge, luminous fronds swaying in windless air, their veins lit from within by Lunehymn and something warmer.

She has given them names, all of them. She talks to them when the silence becomes too much.

"You're growing crooked," she tells one, brushing a warped frond. "Just like me."

Her laughter is a thin, wet sound that vanishes into the open sky.

She has been shaping birds.

In the atelier—a long gallery opening off the crescent court, its translucent walls veined with slow-swirling light—birds fill every surface. Birds with wings too tight. Birds with no wings at all. Birds frozen in the moment of falling. Birds almost remembering how to fly. Workbenches, plinths, windowsills: every edge and ledge hosts another attempt.

She has been singing. The lullaby of Mira—the one that became Lacrimosa's anchor—because it is the only song that makes her feel less alone. She does not know whose voice she hears when she sings it. Hers? Mira's? Lacrimosa's? The tones have worn into each other in the long dreaming until she no longer trusts where she begins.

She has been dancing, sometimes, when the stillness presses too hard.

In the concerto hall at the far end of the crescent—a high, vaulted chamber whose outer wall is nothing but open arches—polished stone forms a wide, bare floor, marked with faint circular inlays where her steps once traced spell-patterns. A pale violin rests on a stand with stellar sheets at the center of the room, its wood the color of [[Glimmerfern]]; its strings catch the moonlight and answer with a quiet hum even when untouched. From here, the view drops straight over the gardens and into the dark: Glimmerfern terraces, the spill of Lunehymn, the curve of the veiled dome at the edges where space bends wrong.

This hall was a gift and a prison she did not know how to accept at first. A hall to dance, a violin to play, a view of the gardens she could coax to life. A place to keep doing what she was made for, even inside a cage.

Magic lives in movement and music for her. It coils in her ankles, in the turn of her wrists, in the bow when it bites the strings, and Selenea has been pacing tonight.

When she feels the Aria approaching—through the Lunehymn running beneath her skin, through the dream-tide, through the Fundamental Frequency she has not felt in eons but would know in any guise—she freezes.

Her heart, which she has spent centuries ignoring, slams against her ribs.

She does not think: What will I say?

She thinks: Is that—? She’s here. She’s actually here. This is not a dream. This is not a dream—

The Aria manifests at the threshold of the crescent.

She steps out of a shimmer of folded light at the far end of the colonnade, above the central lobby where pillars ring the fountain. She wears the mortal body of the Eighth Cycle: with her signature yet simple black nebulae dress, bare feet, golden hair fading to pinks and softer hues, loose and tangled. She looks fragile beneath the tall pillars and the open stars, framed by the arc and by the distant, shifting [[Auric Geometry]] of the prison veil.

For a moment she only stands there, trembling on the arch above the gardens, as if afraid to set foot on the crescent proper.

Before Auric Aria can begin wandering the galleries and corridors, Selenea is already moving. Not walking—lunging. She crosses the court in three stumbling strides, shoulder clipping a plinth, one half-formed sculpture shattering and silver threads scattering underfoot. Her soles slide on the slick residue of her own tears made solid on the polished stone.

The Aria's mouth opens as she sees her.

"Selenea, I—"

"No."

She crashes into the Aria.

Her arms lock around her—too tight, desperate, shaking. She buries her face in the hollow of the Aria's shoulder, breathing in the old, impossible familiarity of her, where warmed skin and faint Lunehymn meet.

"No," she says again, muffled. "Don't. Don't apologize. Don't explain. Don't say anything yet."

The Aria stands frozen. Her arms hang at her sides. Her mouth is still open, the apology half-formed and stranded on her tongue.

Selenea pulled back just enough—not to look at the Aria's face, but to look at her collarbone, her throat, the place where her mortal pulse beats. Not her eyes. Not yet.

"I need to say this first," Selenea says. Her breath hitches. "I need to say this before I see your face. Because if I see your face I might forget."

The Aria's fingers twitch, but she does not move.

"I was so confused," Selenea says. "For so long. And then I was sad. And then I was nothing. And then I was dreaming again."

She laughs—a small, broken sound.

"Do you know what I dreamed about most? Not escape. Not revenge. Not even you apologizing or shattering the veil."

Her voice drops to barely a whisper.

"I dreamed about your hands."

The Aria's breath stops.

"Your hands," Selenea repeats. "The way they moved when you wove. The way they trembled when you were uncertain. The way they reached for mine when we were creating together." A softer laugh escapes her. "I shaped them out of light a hundred times. A thousand. But I could never get the trembling right."

She finally—finally—looks up. Into the Aria's eyes.

"You're trembling even now."

The Aria's voice is barely audible. "Always…"

"I know." Selenea's hands slide up to her shoulders, gripping. "I know."

The Aria's lips part. "Selenea, I—"

"You did."

The Aria blinks. "What?"

"You did." Selenea's voice is softer now, but something almost playful threads through it. "You hurt me. I tried so desperately to pass your test, and breach the veil."

[[Selenea]] voice drops to a whisper.

"But it was never a test was it? You left me... here for eons."

The Aria bows her head. "No... it wasn't."

"I don't forgive you."

The Aria's voice is a thread. "I know."

"I don't know if I ever will."

The Aria nods, chest tightening around a breath that will not come smoothly, as she begins to have teary eyes. "I... know…"

Selenea is silent for a moment.

Then she reaches out and pokes the Aria's chest, right over her heart.

"But I might."

The Aria looks up, startled.

"If—" Selenea's lips twitch. "If you stop looking at me like a kicked puppy. It's annoying. You're supposed to be the Golden Sovereign. At least pretend to have some dignity."

The Aria blinks. "I—what?"

"You heard me." Selenea’s smile wobbles, but it’s real. "I have been waiting eons for you to show up, and the first thing you do is stand there shaking and waiting for me to yell at you." She shakes her head. "And you didn't even bring me a gift."

The Aria's mouth opens. Closes. Opens again.

"A—a gift?"

"Yes. A gift." Selenea gestures at the expanse of the crescent court, and the distant shimmer of the veiled dome. "I've been trapped here for ages. Do you know how boring it gets? I would have settled for a nice rock. A shiny rock. Something with a little effort. Or, I don’t know, proper tools for the gardens."

The Aria stares at her. Her trembling has not stopped, but something in her expression is shifting—confusion, disbelief.

"Selenea, I—I don't understand. You said you don't forgive me."

"I don't." Selenea's voice softens. "Not yet. Maybe not for a long time. But I also don't want to spend what little time we have screaming at you." She shrugs. "I've done enough of that to the pillars. They're excellent listeners, but terrible at talking back."

The Aria gives a startled, broken laugh. Raw. "You're—you're joking."

"More than that, I am me." She says it like it explains everything. "I am always joking. It's how I survive."

She takes the Aria's hands—still hanging uselessly—and folds them between her own.

"How long are you going to take to hug me back?"

The Aria startles, then moves clumsily, wrapping her arms around Selenea in a hesitant, earnest embrace.

Selenea is silent for a long moment, pressing the Auric Aria close before finally letting her go.

"The prison isn't ugly," she says.

The Aria draws back, startled. "What?"

The Aria flinches. "Selenea—"

"The prison." Selenea’s thumbs brush away the first glitter of golden tears. "You built me a prison. But you didn't build me a dungeon."

She turns, gesturing around them.

"You built me a palace. A crescent open to the sky. Gardens hanging beneath. An atelier full of tools. A concerto hall with a view. Inner chambers where I can sleep and dream. A place with light and air and—" she gestures at the fountain, at the Lunehymn streaming in silver rivulets through the stone, spilling over the inner curve toward the gardens—"and beauty."

The Aria's breath stops.

"I—I don't—"

"No, listen." Selenea's grip tightens on her hands. "I heard how you called it a cage. You spent eons believing you had locked me in a dungeon."

She looks around the moon-palace. At the translucent walls banded with gentle luminance, at the pillars, the veiled dome curving at the far edges, the terraces below flush with Glimmerfern, the sculptures gathered in the atelier, the birds.

She points toward the open arches that lead to the concerto hall.

"But it's not ugly. You let me keep music."

The Aria's breath catches.

"It's not ugly," Selenea repeats. "You could have put me anywhere. In the dark. In the cold. In a box so small I couldn't move. You could have left me with bare stone and silence." She shakes her head. "But you didn't. You gave me this."

She points toward the Lunehymn fountain, where silver water falls in continuous sheets, feeds the crescent channels, and then thins to a rain over the gardens.

And the Aria's gaze drifts. 

Past Selenea's shoulder. To the fountain behind her. To the flowing rivers of Lunehymn falling in silver sheets across the stairs, tracing the crescent's curve, gardens hanging below in terraces of Glimmerfern. And a stage burrowed by the gleam of [[Elemental Sprite]]s mixing with the dandling starlight of [[Cosmic Motion]].

The Aria's voice fractures. "I—I forgot I had built this—"

Selenea follows her gaze, then returns to her face directly looking at her awestruck wonder until something softens.

"Why?" Selenea searches the Aria's face. "Why did you make it beautiful? You didn't have to. I wouldn't have known. I couldn't have stopped you."

The Aria's still-trembling hands tighten around Selenea's.

"I may have forgotten the interior, but I remember the feeling. I couldn't bear to hurt you more than I already had," She says in a whimper.

"Because I knew—even then, even when I was at my worst—that you deserved better. And I couldn't give you better. But at least I could give you this."

She looks around the crescent, at the gardens, the atelier, the soft glow of Lunehymn along the channels.

"I remember. That I could give you something more than a room that felt like a cage. Even if it was one."

A pause. A quieter admission.

"Even if I didn't remember what it was."

Selenea stares at her with the glee she first had when she first met the [[Auric Aria]].

"You cared," she says. Not a question.

The Aria nods. A tear of Golden Ichor slips down her chin.

"I have always…"

Selenea is quiet for a long moment.

Then she laughs—that same wet, fractured sound, but warm now.

Her hands frame the Aria's face.

"You stupid, stupid woman."

Selenea's own eyes overflow, but she forces words through the shaken breathing.

"You are the worst jailor in history."

The Aria blinks. "What?"

"You built me a beautiful prison and then hated yourself for it for eons." Selenea shakes her head. "That's not how jailors work. You're supposed to be cruel. You're supposed to enjoy it. Not even in my weirdest dreams couldn't I even imagine one who would build me an island in the firmament."

The Aria's voice is barely audible. "I never… enjoyed—"

"I know." Selenea squeezes her hands. "That's what kept me hoping."

She tugs the Aria gently toward the inner curve of the crescent, where the court opens into a series of archways leading deeper into the palace.

"Come. If I'm going to call you the worst jailor in history, I might as well show you your crimes And only then, perhaps, you'll fully remember the prison you built."

She leads her first along the colonnade, past the Lunehymn fountain. The silver liquid sings softly as it falls, then splits into slender runnels tracing the stone edge before tumbling in a glittering veil into the gardens below.

"This," Selenea says, nodding toward the cascade, "is what keeps me breathing. Lunehymn from the fountain, through the crescent, down into the roots."

They descend a short stair to the gardens—terraces carved into the inner face of the crescent, heavy with Glimmerfern, pale-leaved shrubs, and strange flowers that open only under moonlight.

"I named them all," Selenea says quietly. "Every fern. Every blossom. For a long time I was sure they were smarter than I was."

She glances at the Aria, half-smiling.

"When I first realized what you’d done, I didn’t hate you. Not at first. I thought I just wasn’t clever enough to understand. That if I had been smarter, I’d see the reason. So I turned every question on myself. Why wasn’t I enough? Why couldn’t I solve the puzzle you left me?"

Her fingers brush a Glimmerfern leaf.

"It took a long time before I let myself be angry at you at all."

They climb back to the crescent court and cross into the atelier: a high, vaulted gallery lined with translucent walls, bathed in the reflected glow of Lunehymn from outside. Workbenches stand in rows, cluttered with tools and shards and silver thread. Sculptures line the walls and crowd the floor.

"The dreamers told me many things," Selenea says. "Even about her. Lacrimosa."

The Aria's breath catches.

"They told me she envied me."

The Aria nods, barely.

"Not my freedom," Selenea continues. "I never had that. She envied my suffering. My imprisonment. My… importance." She laughs softly, disbelieving herself. "She wanted to be me. Trapped in the Moon. Bleeding through the Leylines. Because at least then she would matter."

Selenea is quiet for a long moment.

"I had dreams about the Hollowing," she adds. "She wasn't all wrong."

The Aria looks up sharply. "About what?"

"About being essential." Selenea gestures at the translucent walls, where the Lunehymn glows like veins of silver light. "The Leylines run on my Lunehymn. The world would die without me. I am essential. I do matter." She pauses. "I just didn't know it. Not until she made you look."

The Aria's eyes fill with fresh tears.

"When I learned about her," Selenea says, "when the dreamers told me that another goddess had spent eons wishing she could trade places with me—" She shakes her head. "I didn't know what to feel. I was confused enough already. But there was something else."

She looks at the Aria.

"Hope."

"Hope?" the Aria echoes.

"Because if she was right about one thing—if being essential is worth anything—then maybe I wasn't just abandoned. Maybe I wasn't just the one you forgot." Her voice drops to a whisper. "Maybe… I was the one you treasured. Even if you couldn't show it. Even if you took eons to appear."

She leads Aria along the crescent to the concerto hall up the stairs of the garden.

The chamber opens like a breath. One whole side is arches, columns framing an unbroken view of the gardens and, beyond them, the curving veil of the prison dome and the vast [[Grand Thread Rings]] of the [[Great Harmonic Loom]] below. The floor is polished stone, inlaid with faint circles and lines like the ghosts of spell-diagrams.

At the center of the hall, on a simple stand, rests the violin.

Its wood is pale, almost white, with a soft internal glow—the color of moonlight caught in crystal. Blue and golden filigree traces the edges, forming subtle echoes of the [[Soul Leitmotif]] at her throat. The bow leans beside it, shimmering faintly azure.

Selenea pauses there, letting the Aria look.

"You didn't just give me only rooms," she says quietly. "You gave me this."

She brushes her fingertips along the neck of the violin. The strings hum in response, a low, aching note that ripples through the Lunehymn below and sets the Glimmerfern in the gardens swaying.

"Movement and sound," she says. "In the way I step. In the way I draw the bow. You knew my creation magic. And you still left me the hall. The instrument. The view."

Her mouth twists.

"You could have kept me from casting anything at all, if you’d wanted. You could have left me mute and still. But you didn't. You left me a stage."

She turns, looking out over the gardens.

"Do you remember when they were empty?"

The Aria swallows. "I… remember the stone. The fountain. Not the ferns."

"They weren't here at first," Selenea says. "Just bare terraces and Lunehymn falling into nothing. And then one night—"

She closes her eyes.

"One night something else bled through. Rays of radiance in golden hums. Like the opposite of everything I am."

"Aetherlight," the Aria whispers.

Selenea nods.

"II only felt the surge. It came from below—from you. From the way you looked at the Moon like you wanted to unmake yourself with it."

Her fingers tighten on the violin’s neck.

"It hit the Lunehymn like a chord played too hard. The fountain flared. The channels lit gold. And the terraces—" She gestures down at the gardens. "—and from that burst. Glimmerfern began growing slowly from the stone. All of it at once too. Like something in you broke, and the regret spilled here."

The Aria’s eyes blur with tears.

"So when I say the prison isn't ugly, I don't mean it's enough. I don't mean it's forgiveness. I mean you made it beautiful in all the ways you didn't know how to be to yourself, and I've come to appreciate its [[Auric Geometry]]."

[[Selenea]] gaze softens.

And then—

She stops.

The Lunehymn is very present here. Or perhaps it always has been, and she has only now stopped pretending not to hear it. It runs through the channels beneath the floor of the concerto hall in slow silver rivulets, feeding the gardens below, and where Selenea stands—fingers still resting against the violin's neck, its strings still holding that last aching hum—the sound of it rises through the soles of her bare feet like something being remembered against her will.

She is still standing in the hall.

She is also somewhere else.

There is a version of the crescent she visits without meaning to. She has been there ten thousand times. She knows every stone of it—differently than she knows the real stone, with that particular intimacy that belongs to things that have never existed but are nonetheless perfectly detailed. In that version the terraces are not empty. There is a presence beside her that does not tremble—a warmth with sure hands that moves through Auric Geometry the way water moves through stone channels: without effort, without the drag of fear. And she herself moves differently there. Easily. Without the weight of eons spent talking to plants because nothing else would answer.

They are building something.

She can never see the shape of it. She never can. The fantasy always blurs at the edges where the finished form would be—because she does not know what they were building, only that it was theirs, and it was beautiful, and the birds in the atelier were flying, all of them, every last one, with wings loose as sighs.

In the dream, she understands Auric Geometry.

In the dream, she was always enough.

She does not know how long she stands there.

The Aria's hand closes around hers.

Not gently. Not urgently. Fingers settling between hers the way threads find the right groove. Real. Warm. Imprecise.

Trembling.

Selenea blinks.

The crescent reassembles itself around her—cold stone, the sound of Lunehymn in the actual channels, the real gardens below with their imperfect Glimmerfern and their roots that have no name in any dream. The hall. The stand. The violin, still humming.

The Aria.

Not the dream's hands. These ones. Which tremble.

Selenea exhales.

She does not pull her hand away. She does not look at the Aria. For a moment she only stares at the gardens below, where the real Glimmerfern sways in the windless dark.

"I do that sometimes," she says at last. Quietly. "Still. I was not always—here. Even when I seemed to be."

The Aria does not release her hand.

"I know." A beat. "I could feel it. Through the Lunehymn. The frequency changed."

Selenea turns to look at her.

The Aria's expression is not pity. Selenea has spent eons preparing for pity—a whole architecture of deflection, the jokes, the shrug that means don't look too long. This is not pity. It has more weight in it. Something she recognizes but has not seen on the Aria's face before.

The Aria looks like she is about to confess something.

"I did it too," she says. "Not with Lunehymn. But—" She stops. Starts over, and there is something careful in her voice, the way someone speaks when they are dismantling a version of events they have lived inside long enough to mistake it for fact. 

"I had a story I told myself. At the end of the Eighth Cycle. I was standing at the edge of a cliff, in a crescent field of the Fated Flowers you had ever shown me as hope, and I looked up at the Moon one last time, and I—" She stops again. Something moves across her face — not grief exactly, but the shape grief leaves behind when it has been carried long enough to become landscape. "I told myself you were safe. That the palace was enough. That the silence between us was bearable, if held a certain way."

Her eyes move to the gardens below. To the Glimmerfern she now knows grew from the grief of that very moment.

"It was the last thing I gave myself before I—" The sentence does not finish. She does not finish it. With trembling lips she concludes "It was easier to stay inside the story..."

Selenea watches her.

"I know which side of the veil I was on," the Aria says quietly. "I know what I had that you didn't. I'm not saying it was the same."

"I know you're not," Selenea says.

"But I told myself a version where I was always about to come back." The Aria's jaw tightens slightly. "The next cycle. The next time I had enough certainty. In my version, I was always just—ahead of the moment. I never had to actually arrive."

The Lunehymn pulses. Once. Then settles.

Selenea is quiet for a long moment. Her thumb moves—once, absent—across the back of the Aria's hand.

"What were we doing," she asks at last, "in your story?"

The Aria blinks with tears. "What?"

"In your version. What were we doing when you came back."

Something crosses the Aria's face—caught, a little. The particular vulnerability of admitting to something that seemed, until spoken, like a private thought too fragile for air.

"Creating," she says, finally. Barely above a whisper. "We were always about to create something."

Selenea says nothing.

"I know," the Aria says. "I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like mine." Selenea's voice is very quiet. "Except in mine the birds flew."

The Aria makes a sound—not quite a laugh. Too raw to be one.

"Mine were always almost ready," she says.

Selenea looks down at their hands. At the trembling still present in the Aria's fingers, which the dream always got wrong. Which she tried a thousand times to replicate in light and always failed.

"You never shook," she says. "In my version. I could never—" She pauses. "I could shape your hands a hundred times. The angle, the way you held the thread. But I could never get the trembling right."

The Aria goes very still.

"I'm sorry," she says. Softer than the earlier apology. "That my real version—" She stops. "That I'm harder than the one you practiced."

Selenea looks up at her.

For a long moment she says nothing. Then, slowly, something shifts in her face—not quite a smile, but rather in the way a note resolves without quite landing.

"I prefer the real one anyway,"

"The dream one," Selenea says, "never actually showed up."

She turns, still holding the Aria's hand, and begins to lead her back along the crescent.

"Come. I want to show you the rest."

She leads the Aria back along the crescent to the gardens, descending the stone steps.

They arrive deeper into the atelier, where rows of sculptures assemble a history only she has witnessed.

"Look," she says, stopping before a cluster of figures. "These are the creatures we made together. In the Sixth Cycle. The ones that lived. The ones that died. The ones that became monsters."

She trails her fingers along the flank of a small horned creature frozen mid-leap.

"I recreated them. All of them. Every species we ever shaped. Every life we breathed into being." Her voice softens. "I wanted to remember. Even the ones that suffered. Even the ones that perished. They were ours. I couldn't bear to let them vanish."

The Aria reaches out, touching the same sculpture. "You remembered," she whispers.

"Of course I remembered." Selenea’s voice sharpens. "I was here. Even when you weren't. Even when you fell silent and left me alone. I remembered everything."

She pulls the Aria further along the gallery.

"These are the birds," she says, gesturing at the shelves lined with winged figures. "I told you I have been practicing. Every day. For eons. Trying to get the wings right."

She picks up one of the newer birds, its wings still clenched close to its body, and holds it out.

"See? Still wrong. I could never get them to loosen. I could never make them fly."

The Aria takes the sculpture. Her fingers trace its rigid wings.

"Because you were shaping them alone," she says. "The wings need two hands."

Selenea nods. "I know. That's why I kept making them. Hoping that someday—"

"Someday I would come back," the Aria finishes.

"Someday you would come back."

They stand together in the silver-lit atelier, surrounded by thousands of birds, all waiting.

Selenea guides her at last into the innermost chamber that opens off the gallery: a smaller room with a single broad workbench beneath a high oculus where moonlight falls in a steady column. Here the Lunehymn channel runs in a quiet curve along the perimeter, a continuous pulse of light against the stone.

Selenea clears a space on the crowded workbench among the clutter—pushing aside half-finished sculptures, scattering threads—and sets down a different bird. This one is older. Its wings are only suggested. Its surface is dull with age. 

"This one," she says. "Do you remember it?"

The Aria stares. Confusion rises, then recognition, then something raw.

"The Sixth Cycle," she whispers. "We were—"

"We were creating together," Selenea finishes. "You were weaving the body. I was shaping the wings. And then—"

"Then I left."

"You left." Selenea's voice is steady now, though her hands tremble. "You didn't say goodbye. You didn't explain. You just left. In tears, in the middle of the bird. In the middle of us."

The Aria reaches for the sculpture. Her fingers hover over the unfinished wings.

"I couldn't—" she starts, shaking. "I was so afraid. The rotting was everywhere, and everything we made was dying. And I thought—"

"You thought you were the problem."

The Aria nods.

"You thought if you stayed, you would make it worse."

"Yes."

"So you left."

"Yes."

Selenea holds the bird between them and steadies the Aria's hand.

"This is what you left behind," she says. "Not just me. This. The bird we never finished. The wings you never trusted me to shape."

"I trusted you," the Aria whispers. "I—"

"Then why did you leave?"

The question hangs in the column of light, in the quiet murmur of Lunehymn.

The Aria has no answer.

Selenea looks down at the bird. At its blank wings. At the place where the Aria's thread stopped and her own began.

"I will not forgive you," she says again. "Not for the prison. Not for the eons of silence."

And she looks up. A smile tugs at the corner of her mouth—shaky, but teasing before more tears shimmer in the Aria's eyes.

"But I will forgive you—"

She pauses. The Aria holds her breath.

"—if you finish this bird with me."

The Aria's eyes widen.

"Not because I want to forget," Selenea says. "Not because I want to pretend the past didn't happen. But because this—" she lifts the bird slightly—"this is what we were building. Before everything. Before the fear, and this whole contraption of pillars and veils. This."

She presses the bird into the Aria's hands.

"You left it unfinished. You left me unfinished. And I have been waiting—not for an apology, not for an explanation, but for this. For you to pick up the thread and finish what we started."

She meets the Aria's eyes.

"So. What do you say? Want to build a bird?"

Before the Aria can find her voice, Selenea stands—still holding her hand—and guides her to stand beside the workbench.

"Here," she says. "We start with this one."

The Aria looks down at the bird. At the wings waiting to be shaped.

"I don't—" she starts. "I don't know if I remember how."

Selenea takes her hands—still trembling—and sets them upon the bird's wings.

"Then I will remind you."

She positions the Aria's fingers. Adjusts the angle of her palms.

"Looser," she says. "Trust the light. You always did the wings too tight."

The Aria lets out a startled, wet laugh. "You said that before."

"I have been saying it for eons. To myself, to empty air, to birds that would not fly." Her voice gentles. "I was practicing. Hoping that someday I would get to say it to you again."

She lays her own hands over the Aria's.

"Together," she says. "Like we used to."

They begin to weave.

At first it is clumsy. The Aria's thread stutters with her shaking. Selenea's light flickers, wary of overwhelming. But slowly, painfully, they find their rhythm. The Lunehymn in the channels keeps a steady pulse, guiding them.

The bird's wings begin to open.

Not perfect. Not finished. But moving.

"See?" Selenea murmurs. "That's it. That's how it's supposed to feel."

"I had forgotten," the Aria breathes. "The way it feels to create with you."

"I know."

Tears fall from the Aria's lashes onto the bird's wings, where they mingle with Selenea's silver light.

"Selenea—"

"Don't." Selenea's voice is soft. "Just weave."

They weave.

The bird's wings loosen. The silver glow brightens. For a moment—just a moment—the bird shivers on the edge of flight.

Then the golden thread at the threshold of the palace pulses.

The Aria's form flickers.

"The relic," she says in panic. "It is weakening. I have to—"

"I know." Selenea's grip tightens. "Go. Weave more. Come back."

The Aria nods. She doesn't release the bird until the last instant.

"Next time," she says, "we will finish it. This bird. The one we started in the Sixth Cycle. We will finish it together."

Selenea's smile trembles, but holds. "I will hold you to that."

The Aria's form flickers again. She is turning translucent, the golden thread pulling her back toward the world beyond the veiled dome.

"Selenea—"

"I know."

"I am sorry."

"I know."

"I—"

Selenea leans forward and presses a kiss to her forehead. Soft. Brief. Fierce.

"Go," she says. "Create. And come back."

The Aria's eyes flood—gold and silver mingling.

"Next time," she whispers.

"Next time."

The golden thread pulls. The Aria dissolves into light.

Selenea watches until the last flicker of gold fades from the moon-palace.

Only then does she reach for the bird—the one they were shaping together—and hold it to her chest.

"Next time," she whispers.

The Lunehymn in the walls and channels pulses—softer now, but warmer.

She is still hoping.

The golden thread at the threshold dims, then steadies. The palace is empty again—except for Selenea, sitting on the workbench beneath the oculus, the bird cradled in her hands.

She looks toward the unseen presence beyond the veiled dome.

"Thank you," she says. "For making her come back."

She pauses.

"Tell her—" She stops. Shakes her head. "No. She will hear it from me. Next time."

She smiles—a small, trembling smile, the smile of someone who has been practicing hope for eons and is finally allowed to use it.

"Next time."

She looks down at the bird. At its wings—still incomplete, still waiting.

But looser now.

Learning.

"Next time," she says again.

The thread pulses

Once,  

Twice,

Then settles into a soft, steady glow.

And at last Selenea drops to her knees on the crescent stone. 

Still clutching the bird, and cries.

### **Part II. The [[Pale Moonlit Violin]] Concert**

The Lunehymn channels had been scrubbed clean three times in the space of a single cycle. Not by the sprites — though she had put them to work on the Glimmerfern terraces, and they had done their part without complaint, moving in that familiar choreography of silent service she had long stopped mistaking for company — but by Selenea herself, bare-kneed on the polished stone, working the silver runnels until they ran without hesitation. 

She had needed something to do with her hands. She had needed the kind of task where the work was visible in the result, where you could look behind you and see the difference between where you had been and where you were going.

The hall needed to be right. Not presentable — she had crossed into presentable three rounds of polishing ago. Right. The kind of right that holds when someone is actually watching.

She had spent the intervals between preparations filling the hall.

Not intentionally — or not consciously, the distinction having blurred over cycles between what she meant to do and what simply happened while her hands were occupied and her mind was elsewhere. What she could say, standing now at the entrance of the concerto hall and surveying the rows of figures that occupied the chairs she had arranged in curved arcs from the performance space to the back wall, was that she had been busy.

The hall held an audience. Dozens of them. Woven forms — silver thread drawn through Lunehymn and set into shape, the same technique as the birds, the same patience — each one posed in a chair she had dragged from every corridor of the palace. They were not solid the way stone is solid. They had a translucency, a faint inner luminescence where the channels still ran through them, cold light moving in slow pulses beneath the thread like a second kind of breathing. 

When the moonlight shifted, they shifted with it. In the dark hours before the visit, Selenea had sometimes looked up from her rehearsal and thought, for just a moment, that the nearest one had leaned slightly forward.

She had given them faces.

This was the part she could not explain without it sounding worse than it was. The faces had come from the Lunehymn communion — from the long, deep immersions in the channels when the dreamers found her, when the world below bled upward through the silver current in fragments of sleep and memory and the confused inner weather of creatures she had never seen with her own eyes. 

She had learned the shape of them that way: through frequency first, then impression, then something closer to image — not precise, but present. A jaw she had encountered three times in three different dreams. The specific way a certain brow creased when someone was afraid. The set of a mouth she had caught once in the deep tide, too briefly, and spent six cycles trying to reconstruct from what remained.

She had built the faces from what she could hold.

Some of them were close. The figure in the third row, second from the left — the one she had named first, whom she spoke to when she needed to think out loud — had a face Selenea had been refining for a long time. 

A dreamer who returned with particular frequency, whose emotional signature was warm and considered, who felt in the Lunehymn the way sunlight would feel if sunlight had a temperament. She had given the figure a slight smile: not decided upon but remembered, drawn from the texture of that frequency as faithfully as she could render it in thread. The smile was knowing. Whether it was actually the dreamer's smile or simply the quality Selenea most wanted in the room when she was uncertain, she had stopped trying to determine.

The small figure five rows back — shorter, lighter in the weaving — had a face she had built from a different kind of dreaming. The frequency of children came through the Lunehymn differently than adults: brighter, more chaotic in harmonic, the emotional signal unfiltered.

She had felt it dozens of times, always briefly. The figure's proportions were slightly off — the eyes set fractionally too wide, the hands a little larger than they should be — because she had only ever had moments. She had never had long enough to be sure. The hollow quality of its eyes was not intended. It was the honest result of not being able to complete something she had not had enough of to complete from. She had placed it five rows back, off-center, because it deserved a clear sightline and she could not bear to put it in the front where she might look at its eyes too directly during the third movement.

There was a figure in the left-hand aisle she had mounted on a stand with its arms open, angled slightly as though mid-movement or mid-announcement. 

She had built it after a dream of someone with a very particular quality of urgency, someone who had reached upward through the Lunehymn current with a feeling she could only describe as needing to be heard. The face was imperfect — the nose placed a fraction too low, the chin slightly long — because the dreamer had only passed through her once, and what she had caught was the feeling, not the features. She had done what she could. She had given the face its urgency, at least. That much she had felt clearly.

The newer additions were less settled in their likenesses. There was a figure in row seven whose expression she had not yet finished — the jaw was right, drawn from a dreamer who came through regularly, heavy and deliberate in frequency, but the space above the brow still lacked something she had not yet received from the communion. She had placed her in row seven anyway. Unfinished faces deserved to witness. She had said this to the figure while positioning the chair, and the figure had not disagreed.

Thirty-seven new figures since the last visit. Thirty-seven faces built from the long drift of the Lunehymn, from the fragments of the world below moving through her channels without knowing they passed through her at all.

"They don't know I'm here," she had said to the first figure, one evening, adjusting the chair. "The dreamers, I mean. They come through the Lunehymn and they dream their small dreams and they don't know that I —" She had stopped.

The Lunehymn in the floor ran silver and patient beneath her feet. "I've been building them for eons. I keep thinking if I get enough of them right, I'll understand the whole." She smoothed the figure's shoulder, where the silver thread had loosened slightly at the joint. "I probably won't. I have most of a jaw and the feeling of a name and I've never once seen any of them with my own eyes. But I keep trying. What else am I going to do?"

The figure's slight smile held its single quality steadily.

"Exactly," Selenea said.

The single chair was different.

It stood at the optimal distance from the performance space — not so close that the resonance during the third movement would overwhelm, not so far that the fourth movement would dissolve in the air between them before arriving. The velvet was the color of deep water. 

She had made it herself, in the atelier, over an afternoon she did not entirely remember because she had been thinking about something else. About the last visit. About whether this time, if she played well enough — not for evidence, not to prove she hadn't decayed, but well enough, truly, the way she had played in the Sixth Cycle when creating still felt like breathing — whether something might shift between them.

She had been moving the chair for three hours.

"No," she said, stepping back, tilting her head. "That's worse. If you're sitting there the light from the third arch will cross your face during the entire third movement, and I need to see your face during the third movement." She moved it two inches to the left. Studied it. "Still wrong." Two inches back. Then three to the right. 

The sightline opened toward the dark of the open arches and the curve of the veiled dome and the stars bending wrong at the edges — better — no, worse — she moved it back a single inch and stood with her hands on her hips.

From the third row, the figure with the knowing smile offered nothing useful.

"I know what you're thinking," Selenea said.

She didn't know what the figure was thinking. She had built the figure's expression herself. Whatever knowing lived in that face had come from a dreamer she had tried to faithfully render, and whatever the dreamer had actually felt in the moment that feeling passed through the Lunehymn was something Selenea had no way to verify. The smile meant what Selenea needed it to mean. This was both the thing that made it sustaining and the thing that made it, quietly, insufficient.

"I've moved it fourteen times," she said. "I'm aware. But the geometry keeps —" Almost a laugh. "I keep thinking there's a right answer and I keep failing to arrive at it. That's very consistent with my general relationship to Auric Geometry."

"She could probably calculate the optimal position in twelve seconds," Selenea said to herself as she moved the chair one inch to the left. "There. Fourteen. That's the one."

The chair sat in the center of the curved rows — the only empty seat among the dozens of luminous figures, the single space in the hall that waited without a face above it.

For a long moment she only looked at it.

The Lunehymn changed in the small hours before dawn.

Not dramatically — it never did, with the Aria. No surge, no wave of static, nothing that announced itself. The channels simply recognized something. A harmonic, low and precise, with the quality of something geometric arriving: deliberate, each note placed with the care of a being for whom every frequency meant something. The kind of presence that moved through the world the way water moved through precisely-carved channels.

Selenea had been in the middle of the transition between the second and third movements — that difficult seam where memory tips into rage without warning, where the bow hand has to hold two incompatible things at once — when the floor shifted under her feet. Warmer. A harmonic she had trained herself not to look for between visits, because looking for it between visits was a practice that led somewhere she could not afford to go with any regularity.

She was already across the hall before she had consciously acknowledged what she was moving toward.

She clipped the second row in passing. One of the newer figures lurched on its stand, the silver thread at its shoulder catching the movement; she steadied it with one hand without breaking stride, and it resumed its posture, its imprecise face tipped slightly toward the performance space as if nothing had happened.

Through the arch at the far end of the crescent, the threshold resolved: light folding inward, the familiar form assembling from it — barefoot again, the black nebulae dress settling against pale stone, golden hair falling loose and fading through softer registers, as though the gold were only the most recent version of something that had been many colors before.

The Aria stood at the arch above the gardens with that particular quality she always had upon arriving: contained, uncertain of her own dimensions, standing at the edge of the crescent as though permission had not yet been granted. 

She was still taking shape. Still arriving, beginning to form something considered — the careful architecture of an apology Selenea could hear before it landed, the kind that had been building for cycles, in the way the Aria always prepared herself for difficult things —

Selenea tackled her before it could.

Her arms locked around the Aria's shoulders. The Aria's breath stopped. Her arms hung for one beat, then came up — better than last time, less like someone afraid of breaking what they held. Selenea pressed her forehead against the familiar warmth of her and said nothing for a moment, only held the fact of her. 

The way you hold something whose absence you have run your hands over so many times that you know its shape by what isn't there.

Then she pulled back. Not far. Just enough.

"There was a next time. You really came!!"

"I've missed you too," the Aria said, and it was simple.

"I have a concert for you," Selenea said, glancing back at the hall — at the rows of luminous figures, silver thread catching moonlight in cold slow pulses, the dreamer-faces turned toward the performance space, the imprecise brows and the carefully-rendered mouths and the figure in row seven whose expression was still unfinished. The single velvet chair waiting at the center of all of it. "I've been preparing it for three cycles. There are four movements. The first is longing. The second is memory. The third is rage." A beat. "I haven't finished the fourth."

"What is it supposed to be?" the Aria asked, carefully.

Selenea looked at her directly.

"I don't know yet. I thought I did. I thought it was forgiveness. But then you came back, and I realized —" Something caught in her throat, not quite a laugh. "I don't know what forgiveness sounds like. I've been rehearsing it for eons, but I invented the sound of it, and I don't know if what I invented sounds like the real thing or just like what I needed it to." She shook her head. "I need to play it and find out."

The Aria was looking at the hall. At the figures arranged in their curved rows, the soft internal luminescence of the Lunehymn still moving through them, the dreamer-faces turned attentively toward the open space. Her expression shifted in a way Selenea could not entirely read.

"The faces," the Aria said, very quietly.

"From the Lunehymn," Selenea said, turning to look at the hall alongside her. "I've been collecting them for a long time. When the dreamers come through the channels — fragments, impressions, the feeling of someone rather than the full shape of them — I hold whatever I catch and I build what I can from it." 

Her voice stayed even. "Some of them are close. Some of them are —" She gestured toward the figure in row seven, its unfinished brow. "I've never seen them. I only know them from the inside of what they feel when they sleep. I do the best I can."

The Aria did not speak.

The Aria's expression opened. She was about to reply, but she figured she did not try to answer beautifully, and instead she did not try to redeem herself in a single sentence. She only remained there, still kneeling, still wrecked, still present.

Selenea looked at her and felt that hope again—uncertain, unfinished—not exactly forgiveness, but definitely the first step of the movement she failed to compose in the concert. That particular weight of the bow was the same as two broken things deciding to remain in the same room.

Outside the open arches, the [[Glimmerfern]] continued to sway in the windless dark—the way it had always swayed, with the faithfulness of something that has never been asked to understand what it's responding to, only to respond.

Inside the hall, the Lunehymn ran on through silver channels reflecting the cosmos of the stars, and it concentrated with the same density of the nebulae of the [[Auric Aria]]'s black dress.

Selenea looked at the Aria through tears she had stopped trying to hide.

Then she let her hand fall, finally, toward the Aria's.

The hall empties as every sculpture of [[Lunehymn]] finally melts back into silvery pools, and the [[Elemental Sprite]]s disperse.

Only the two of them remain. 

Only the violin was still there, on its stand at the center of the hall.

Selenea looked at it. Then at the Aria.

"Play something for me." She said pointing at the violin.

The Aria looked at the violin the way someone looks at a word in a language they once knew and have since learned to live without. A long pause. Then she crossed to it, lifted it with the careful, foreign handling of someone for whom instruments have always meant something different—the wrong instrument, held the right way for the wrong reasons, bow angled with too much reverence in the wrong places.

She drew it across the strings.

What came out was not music. It was the idea of music, badly translated. A note that reached for something and missed. A scrape. Two uncertain tones that found each other and immediately lost interest. The Lunehymn flickered in the channels—politely, diplomatically.

Selenea listened with her eyes closed.

When the sound stopped, the silence that followed was the kind that follows something real, if not beautiful.

"That," She said, "was terrible." [[Selenea]] now laughing.

The Aria set the bow down against the stone. A small, caught sound escaped her as a laugh.

"I know."

Selenea opened her eyes. She looked at her for a long moment—at the bow still in her hands, at the instrument that had never belonged to her, at the name she carried like a key to a door she never seemed to remember she was already standing inside.

"You were the singer of the two for a reason" Selenea said, with the softness of a nimble laugh. "Aria."

She let the name sit between them, doing all the work it needed to do.

"Then sing for me, please. I miss that voice." [[Selenea]] replied in a plea.

The Aria was quiet for a moment, flinching at the request.

When she spoke, her voice carried something careful in it—not refusal, but the honesty of someone taking stock of a room they haven't entered in a very long time.

"It's been so long," She said. "I'm not sure I remember how."

Selenea looked at her.

"Next visit, I'll try. I promise."

A beat of silence. Selenea's mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the shape one makes before the armor decides whether to hold.

"Fine. I'll wait," Reluctantly replied. "I've got the patience of waiting for eons, and the birds are going nowhere."

It was a joke. It was also the truest thing she had said all night. The Aria heard both.

Neither of them moved for a moment. Then Selenea's head found its way back against her, and the Aria's arm settled around her, and the violin lay between them on the stone—still warm, still wrong, still somehow exactly what it needed to have been.

Then the golden tether pulled.

She felt it in the Lunehymn before she saw it—the Aria's frequency beginning its slow recession, the warmth in the channels thinning at its edges the way a voice thins at the very end of a held note when the body finally has to breathe.

Once, the Aria's form flickered.

And then she was gone.

Selenea did not move.

Head still angled toward the space where the Aria had been, hands loose in her lap. And she fell asleep, in her dreams she still held to the shape of the visit the way dreams hold what the waking self refuses to release—vivid, particular, unverifiable.

She could feel it: the palace's long slow current pressing up through the stone, through the channels, through the frequency that had never learned to distinguish between her sleeping and her waking. 

She was still dreaming her. The presence persisted. She did not release it.

And the Lunehymn heard.

It changed first the way elegant things change, without announcement. The pale silver of the channels deepened—imperceptibly, and then more, and then past any register she had ever known it reach. Past the slow luminescence she could name by the soles of her bare feet and the rhythm of eons. Past silver entirely.

What remained in the channels was blue. Not the blue of sky or distance. The blue of the sky seen from beneath the sea—a midnight-ocean depth that held no morning in it, suffused from within by something cold and intermittent and ancient.

The bright motes drifting mirrored that of [[Elemental Sprite]]s, tracing paths older than the hall. Stars, suspended. The cosmos, distilled to its own reflection.

The Lunehymn had become a mirror. In that mirror: the hall, the dreamer-figures, the violin still on the stone, the empty velvet chair in its final position—and beyond them all, the firmament, and beyond the firmament, something further in that had not yet acquired the name of event but was becoming one.

It gathered.

The concentrated blue pressed to the edges of its carved channels with the absolute patience of water that has found a seam—not urgency, only gravity, only the irreversible instruction of a goddess who had not released her dreaming.

The Glimmerfern below fell silent.

The star-motes moved their cold paths through the depth.

And the Lunehymn—pooling now, pressing now, dark and mirror-deep and full of something that had been waiting long enough to forget the waiting—reached the edges of the channels.

And passed them in a massive surge beneath the staircase of the lowest level of the [[Parlor of the Moon]].

Large bursts of viscous [[Lunehymn]] were overflowing to the infinite below.

And these clots, parted the sky.
